The printed record

What the early printings say, what they do not, and how they differ

The earliest printed Sawney Beane found so far is in a London book of criminal lives, credited to a Captain Alexander Smith. Its catalogue date is 1726. The contents list him as executed with his wife, eight sons, six daughters, eighteen grandsons and fourteen granddaughters. The book’s title page describes its lives as “taken out of the Records of Newgate”; that is a claim on a title page, and the chapter on Sawney Beane cites no source of its own. Smith’s chapter is headed “Robber, Murderer, and Cannibal”. It places the story in the reign of King James the First, “before he came to the Crown of England”, with the cave in “Shire Galloway”. The family lived there upwards of twenty-five years, and the victims come to about a thousand. The places named include Glasgow, Edinburgh and Leith. Every victim stays unnamed.

A catalogue date of 1734 goes with a general history of highwaymen and murderers, published under the name of a Captain Charles Johnson. “Johnson” is a name on a title page; the episode names no author for it. The figures match Smith’s and the wording does not. Johnson’s account opens with a sentence Smith’s chapter lacks, calling the tale “as well attested as any historical Fact can be” and also “almost incredible”. No witness follows. On page 132, where the account begins, no victim and no village is named, and no year is given. Both dates, 1726 and 1734, are catalogue dates: the English Short Title Catalogue could not be read for this episode.

Johnson sets the family’s beginnings in the reign of Queen Elizabeth and writes that a King James then governed only in Scotland. Read as James the Sixth, that points to before 1603, and an early nineteenth-century crime collection does print James the Sixth. That reading is an inference. In 1843 John Nicholson, gathering tales of the south of Scotland, keeps Johnson’s wording, including “as well attested”, yet his reign is James the First of Scotland, who ruled from 1406 to 1437. If Johnson’s king is James the Sixth, the printed reigns sit more than a century apart, and no source checked gives a reason.

The place differs too. Smith’s cave is in Galloway. Johnson’s page prints “the County of Galway”. Whether that is a printer’s slip or a change, no source seen says. An undated chapbook, catalogued as about 1800, and Nicholson both have Galloway. Today the story is tied to a headland on the Ayrshire coast; no early printing checked names it, and the earliest found on that coast is a novel, The Grey Man, by S. R. Crockett, in 1896. The early printings call him only Sawney. A first name for him, and a name for his wife, are in none of Smith, Johnson, the chapbook or Nicholson; where they first turn up has not been traced.

Johnson has the family held in Edinburgh’s Tolbooth, the old town jail, then taken to Leith, where they “were all executed without any Process”. By the story’s own telling, there was no trial. That is what the tale says, not a finding, and it does not rule out other papers, such as a warrant or a council entry. A local historian, writing in 2002, reports that a Scottish lawyer and crime writer, in 1933, found no mention of Sawney in print or in manuscript, and the same historian reports Pitcairn’s printed trials silent on the family, the innkeepers’ trial unrecorded, and an expedition by James the Sixth unmentioned. A text search of scanned volumes of Pitcairn’s trials, which print selected cases up to 1624, found no Sawney in the parts checked. The archive catalogues could not be searched for this episode. So no record has been shown to exist.

Two older books sit close to the story, and neither is Sawney Beane. A work under Smith’s name, catalogued as 1719, names two rogues called Sawney, Sawney Cunningham and Sawney Douglas; in the volumes checked it has no Sawney Beane. And an English history of Scotland, printed decades before Smith, gives a few lines to an unnamed thief who lived in a den with his wife and children; the family is put to death. The thief gets no name, and the tale has no sea cave and no royal hunt. Whether that tale has any link to Beane, the sources seen do not settle.

A story of a cannibal family is in print in London by its 1726 catalogue date, and across its later printings the reign and the county are worded differently. The same local historian suggests chapbooks and broadsheets stood behind Smith’s chapter, but names none. No source checked shows what the book’s compiler worked from. What did he rest it on? The chapter gives no answer.

Six points are left open here rather than settled, because the sources read for this episode do not settle them. First, the dates: 1726 and 1734 are catalogue dates, checked in library catalogue records but not in the English Short Title Catalogue, and the chapbook’s date is a catalogue guess. Second, absences: every “not found” rests on the printings and volumes read, mostly as scanned text, which can misread a word, so none is a finding about every copy or every archive. Third, the archives: no Scottish archive catalogue could be searched, so no record series has been shown to be empty. Fourth, the unnamed den tale in the English history of Scotland: the Internet Archive record is dated 1696, but its year was not confirmed from the title page, so the episode speaks no year. Fifth, the authorship of the 1734 book, a name on a title page, which the episode does not settle. Sixth, published scholarship on the origin of the story was not read for this episode, which follows the printed texts themselves; the local historian’s suggestion about chapbooks and broadsheets is an assertion with no cited chapbook.
