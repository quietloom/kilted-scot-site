Episode Seven · Gorbals, Glasgow · 1954

The Gorbals Vampire

Hundreds of children go to a Glasgow cemetery one evening in 1954 to hunt a vampire with iron teeth. No source we found names a victim. The explanations offered at the time arrived without evidence, and what started the hunt is not settled.

The real record

What is actually documented, and what is not

On the evening of Thursday 23 September 1954, hundreds of children went to the Southern Necropolis, a cemetery in Glasgow's Gorbals district. The account below comes from The Bulletin, the Glasgow paper that carried it on its front page the next morning, as quoted by the folklorists Hobbs and Cornwell in 1988. It is the route used here to that text: no 1954 newspaper page was read in full for this episode. According to The Bulletin, the hunt began shortly after school hours, and householders phoned the police about the noise. The children came from all over Hutchesontown, some of them scarcely able to walk, with sticks or stones. They said they were hunting a vampire with iron teeth, and that it had killed and eaten “two wee boys”. The paper reported no injuries, damage or arrests, and said the rain called the hunt off.

The story reached print through a telephone call. A crime reporter rang a police office that evening, heard laughter down the line, and someone said he ought to be told about the vampire. Hobbs and Cornwell give this as indirect speech, not a quotation. The front page of Friday 24 September carried the headline “Police Had To Clear ‘Vampire’ Hunters.” One constable who attended said he felt like the Pied Piper of Hamelin, with children streaming after him, all talking at once; what struck him was that the adults asked whether there was anything in the vampire story. The BBC reported in 2010 that the children had told a constable the vampire was seven feet tall. The Bulletin text as quoted gives no height, and no 1954 source seen gives one.

No source read for this episode names a victim, and no police, council or school record was found in the sources searched for this episode. The BBC, without citing anything, says there were no reports of missing children in Glasgow at the time; that is not a proven absence. The hunt happened again on the Friday evening, and the Daily Mirror front page of Saturday 25 September, in a headline read from an image of the page, put hundreds of children at the cemetery for a second day running. The following Monday The Bulletin ran “Vampire doesn't scare any more.”

The first report offered a single explanation, in its closing line: an “H” film had been showing at a local cinema that week. The reporter, Hobbs and Cornwell say, could not later recall which film. On the Saturday The Bulletin said mothers held comics responsible, and the Daily Record front page ran “Horror film blamed for the vampire.” By the Wednesday a councillor for the district had raised comics, and the Daily Record reported it. The press named one comic, Startling Terror Tales, though it is unclear which paper used the name first. Hobbs and Cornwell read that issue and say it has no vampire, no hunt and no iron teeth. By 1 October the Catholic Observer had said on its front page that comics caused the panic, the earliest unqualified claim they found. In their account the link arrived without any new evidence, though they do not rule out some influence, and they found no sign that anyone tried to identify the children who took part or to ask what they read.

Comics reached Parliament that autumn: on 21 October 1954 members put questions on the subject to the Home Secretary, and the text read does not mention the vampire. In February 1955 the Commons debated a bill on harmful publications. John Rankin, a Glasgow Member of Parliament, used the hunt as his one example. He said a school had “closed for the day” and that word spread “by some system of bush telegraph.” Roy Jenkins, speaking in the same debate, warned in general against inferring a cause. In the debate text checked, the House does not decide that comics explained the hunt. That is a reading of one debate, not of the whole record. Royal Assent for the Children and Young Persons (Harmful Publications) Act is dated 6 May 1955.

The iron teeth may have an older history in the city. In 1902 Hugh Macintosh wrote that children in the east end, near Glasgow Green, had nicknamed one of Alexander Allan's daughters “Jenny with the iron teeth.” She wore a dentist's metal fixings, and the children, he wrote, exaggerated her into “a veritable ogre.” In 1879 the poet Alexander Anderson published “Jenny wi the Airn Teeth”, in which a mother threatens a child with an iron-toothed bogey; Hobbs and Cornwell say it was later anthologised for schools. They connect the poem to the iron teeth of 1954 and call that an inference.

Other readings exist. In a 2016 BBC interview quoted by Mental Floss, Martin Barker, who wrote a history of the British horror comics campaign, pointed to social conditions and a wish to escape them. That is speculation, not evidence of motive. The 1951 census figure above is documented context only.

Four points are left open here rather than settled, because the sources read for this episode do not settle them. First, the 1954 newspapers themselves: every 1954 report above is a second-hand quotation (the Daily Mirror headline alone was read from an image of its front page), and a search of the British Newspaper Archive or the Mitchell Library, and of any Glasgow police or school records, would be the way to check it. Second, the date of the Commons debate: Hansard gives 22 February 1955 and Hobbs and Cornwell's note gives 28 February; the episode says only “February 1955”. Third, the number of nights, which sources give as two or three. Fourth, which paper first named the comic.
