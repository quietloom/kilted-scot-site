Episode Six · Appin · 1752

The Appin Murder

A Crown official is shot in the back in a Highland wood, and six months later a man is hanged for it. The Crown never claimed he fired the gun. It named another man as the shooter, and he escaped. Who really fired has never been established.

The real record

What is actually documented, and what is not

On the evening of Thursday 14 May 1752, Colin Campbell of Glenure was walking through the Wood of Lettermore, in Duror in Appin, when he was shot from behind. He was a factor, an official who collected the rents on estates the Crown had taken from Jacobite landowners after the rising of 1745, and he was walking back from Fort William with his nephew Mungo Campbell, an Edinburgh lawyer, a servant, John Mackenzie, and a sheriff-officer, Donald Kennedy, who was to carry out evictions the next day. In the printed trial, nobody in the party was armed. Mungo Campbell said he saw a man in a short dark coat carrying a gun, too far off to recognise. All three men described one shot. The surgeon found two bullets in the body, one on each side of the spine, and the printed record does not reconcile the two.

The Crown wanted Allan Breck Stewart, a deserter from the government's army and the ward of James Stewart of the Glen, who was seen in the district that day and was never caught. James Stewart, half-brother to the exiled owner of the estate, had gone to Edinburgh that spring to try to stop the evictions, and had lost. He was arrested on 16 May with his son. A Privy Council proclamation of 28 May offered a pardon to any accomplice who informed, “except the person who actually shot” Campbell, and a reward of one hundred pounds per conviction. No informer appears in the surviving papers.

James Stewart was tried at Inveraray in September 1752 as an accessory, “art and part”, with Allan Breck named as the man who fired. The presiding judge was Archibald Campbell, 3rd Duke of Argyll and chief of the Campbell clan. Eleven of the fifteen jurors were Campbells, counted against the printed list, and the clerk to the jury was a former bailie of Inveraray. The printed trial notes that no objection was taken. D. N. Mackay's 1907 edition gives about fifty hours of evidence across two nights, which rests on his figure alone. The jury found James Stewart guilty. He was hanged on 8 November 1752 beside the Ballachulish ferry and his body hung in chains, and in his last words he said he had no previous knowledge of the murder.

  • 15jurors, chosen by the judges, with no ballot; eleven of them Campbells
  • £108 17s 10dthe Crown's bill for the gibbet and its two hangmen, one from Glasgow and one from Inverness
  • 16soldiers, with their officers, guarding the body in December 1752
  • 18 months to 9 yearshow long published accounts say the body hung there; they disagree

The gibbet was plated with iron because the Crown's lawyers feared it might be cut down, according to the Exchequer petition of 1753; the same papers say two hangmen were brought in because Argyllshire had none of its own, and in case the execution failed through bad weather or another accident. The record does not mention friends or a rescue attempt.

Some of the evidence cuts against the Crown's account of the shooter. Mungo Campbell said the man was too far away for him to know him, and two servants put Allan Breck's large Spanish gun at James Stewart's house that day. Mackay's edition notes that Allan, as a deserter, already had a reason to run. Robert Louis Stevenson built Kidnapped (1886) on the case. In its dedication he wrote that the descendants of “the other man” who fired the shot were still in the country, and that his name “you shall not hear”; the same dedication admits how little he was “touched by the desire of accuracy.”

Three theories are in print, and none is verified. An old tradition in Appin, reported in print since 1886, holds that the killer's name is known. In 2001 Anda Penman named a young laird called Donald Stewart, on the strength of one oral source that no one has corroborated; a Donald Stewart does appear in the 1752 record, swearing that he met Allan Breck on the hill that night, but the record does not connect him to the shot. In 2016 the historians Allan MacInnes and Mhairi Livingstone suggested that the shot came from Campbell's own nephew, Mungo Campbell, who became factor within a year; it is a theory about motive and opportunity, and no physical evidence has been put forward for it.

The story says · the record shows

Each claim, and where it actually came from

As it circulatesVerdictIn the record
James Stewart fired the shot. Not the Crown's case The indictment charged him as an accessory, “art and part”, and named Allan Breck as the man who fired. The Crown never claimed Stewart pulled the trigger. Mackay (ed.), Trial of James Stewart, 1907
The man who killed Campbell is known, and the Stewarts kept his name secret. Tradition only An old Appin tradition says so, and Stevenson's dedication repeats the idea. No such claim has ever been verified, and Mackay's own enquiry found only conflicting accounts. Kidnapped (1886); Old Appin; Mackay, Appendix
A young laird, Donald Stewart, fired the shot. One oral source The 2001 claim rests on a single oral source and has not been corroborated. A Donald Stewart is in the 1752 record, swearing he met Allan Breck on the hill; it does not connect him to the shot, and whether he is the same man is unproven. The original 2001 report was not seen. The Scotsman, 2005; Scottish Legal News, 2018
Mungo Campbell fired the shot. Theory, no evidence Put forward in 2016 by Allan MacInnes and Mhairi Livingstone as a theory of motive and opportunity. No physical evidence has been offered. BBC News, 10 June 2016
Campbell was nicknamed the Red Fox. Not established A witness swore Allan Breck asked him to fetch “the red fox's skin” and understood it to mean Campbell only after the murder. The sworn record gives the name “Colin Roy”, Red Colin; whether “Red Fox” was a nickname at the time, or became one later, is not established. Mackay (ed.), deposition of Malcolm MacColl
The body hung on the gibbet for years. Disputed Published accounts give anything from eighteen months to nine years. The Exchequer papers confirm soldiers guarding the body in December 1752; the episode states the dispute and gives no single figure. Millar (ed.), Forfeited Estates Papers, 1909

Three points are left open here rather than settled, because the sources read for this episode do not settle them. First, a request to the Criminal Cases Review Commission to review the case was refused: the application is dated to 2008 only second-hand, and the date of the refusal is not established from anything read. Second, the Scottish Government declined a posthumous pardon in 2015, as reported by Scottish Legal News from the government's own letter. Third, the fifty hours of evidence is Mackay's figure alone.
