Episode Two · Isle of Mull · December 1975

The Great Mull Air Mystery

The inquiry decided he ditched in the sound and swam ashore. The pathologist found no salt water on him at all — and the plane was found twice, in two different states.

The real record

What is actually documented, and what is not

On 20 December 1975 Norman Peter Gibbs travelled to the Isle of Mull with his girlfriend Felicity Grainger to look at hotel investment properties, staying at the Glenforsa Hotel beside its own grass airstrip. Gibbs was 55: a wartime Spitfire pilot with No. 41 Squadron RAF, then a professional violinist — Philharmonia, London Symphony Orchestra, leader of the BBC Scottish Symphony Orchestra from 1960 to 1963 — and latterly a London property developer.

On the morning of 24 December he flew a hired Cessna F150H, registration G-AVTN, from Glenforsa to Broadford on Skye and back. The aircraft belonged to Ian Hamilton QC — the same Ian Hamilton who had taken the Stone of Destiny from Westminster Abbey in 1950.

After dinner that evening Gibbs decided on an unscheduled solo night circuit. The strip was 780m of unlit grass; the night was moonless. Grainger held torches at the runway end. He taxied, paused with the engine running, took off, and was gone within minutes.

Police, RAF and Royal Naval Air Service mounted an extensive land, sea and air search that night and after. Nothing was found. The documented search route passed through the ground where his body would later be lying.

In April 1976 the shepherd Donald MacKinnon found Gibbs about a mile from the airfield, some 400 feet up a hillside, draped across a fallen larch and facing downhill. The body was decomposed but essentially uninjured. The post-mortem in Glasgow is the part of this case that has never been explained away:

  • 1minor cut to one leg — and no injuries whatever consistent with a crash
  • 0alcohol or drugs found
  • 0salt water on the body, and no marine organisms on his clothing or boots
  • 4 molying out — a condition entirely consistent with the time elapsed

The Fatal Accident Inquiry at Oban concluded he had ditched in the Sound of Mull, swum ashore and died of exposure while disoriented. That verdict and that pathology finding do not agree, and the record has never reconciled them.

The plane stayed missing for eleven years. In September 1986 clam-fishing divers found a red and white aircraft about 100 feet down in the Sound of Mull, roughly a mile off the runway approach, carrying the registration G-AVTN. In February 2004 Royal Navy minesweepers relocated wreckage in the same area and put a remote camera on it. The two examinations do not describe the same wreck.

The story says · the record shows

Each claim, and where it actually came from

As it circulatesVerdictIn the record
The plane was never found. False Found twice: by clam divers in September 1986, and again by Royal Navy remote camera in February 2004. Wikipedia; BBC News, 7 Feb 2004
He was flying a Cessna 172. Wrong type A Cessna F150H — a Reims-built Cessna 150 variant — registration G-AVTN. Every source checked agrees. Wikipedia; Fear of Landing
The wreck told a consistent story. Contradicts itself 1986: both wings sheared off. 2004: one wing still attached. Either it changed on the seabed across eighteen years, or the two examinations saw it differently. Wikipedia; BBC News, 7 Feb 2004
The official verdict fits the evidence. Conflicts with its own forensics The inquiry had him ditching and swimming ashore. The pathologist found no salt water and no marine organisms on his clothing or boots. MacAdam 1985, via the Byers biographical compilation
His body was found on Ben Talaidh. Not supported No source names Beinn Talaidh — a separate peak about four miles up Glen Forsa — as the discovery site. Episode research pack §2
He fell from the aircraft in flight. Most repeated, least supported Pilots hold this close to mechanically impossible in a Cessna 150 without deliberate action, and no parachute was found. PPRuNe; Sylvia Wrigley

Two further disputes are left open here rather than settled, because the research did not settle them: how far the body lay from the airfield (one mile in most sources, four in the Mull Directory), and who actually found the 1986 wreck (widely credited to “the Grieve brothers”, possibly a media misattribution). Neither is asserted as fact in the episode.

On the wreck illustrations. The episode's two wreck images are captioned on-frame, for their full duration: Illustrations — AI-generated, not photographs of the actual wreck. They depict the 1986 and 2004 accounts respectively. No photographs of the actual wreck are known to exist; the sourcing pass searched for them specifically and found none, which is why the illustrations exist at all.
