Episode Eight · Loch nan Uamh and Loch Arkaig · 1746

The Lost Gold of Loch Arkaig

Two French ships land money for the Jacobite cause on the west coast, about a fortnight after Culloden. The sums can be followed in the surviving papers. Who received the balance cannot, and the papers disagree about it.

The real record

What is actually documented, and what is not

Culloden was fought on 16 April 1746. About a fortnight later by Murray’s reckoning, so at the end of April (Dr Cameron’s Memorial says the beginning of May), two French ships anchored in Loch nan Uamh, on the west coast of the Highlands, with money for Prince Charles Edward Stuart. John Murray of Broughton, who took charge of it, put the cargo at 35,000 louis d’or, and Dr Archibald Cameron, writing a few years later, gave the same figure. Murray said it came in six casks. The two counts are not independent, since Cameron’s account rests on the money Murray held, and other sources give 36,000 or 40,000. Murray’s memoir was written years afterwards by the man who held the money, and Cameron’s Memorial is read here in a clan archive’s transcript.

Three Royal Navy sloops engaged the French vessels in the loch around 2 May, by one modern account; another gives the 3rd. Dr Cameron’s Memorial, written in Rome around 1750, says the money was being landed during that fight, and that some of it was stolen by a man who then went abroad in one of the French ships. It adds that the priest who heard his confession kept his name secret. The Memorial itself has a page missing, so the start of Cameron’s narrative is lost. Murray tells it differently. He writes that one cask went missing, was found that evening, and was returned the next morning short of one bag, which he put at seven or eight hundred louis. He says the thieves were guessed at from a description, and that two men were tried by court martial. Neither writer names the thief. The Memorial’s own figure is 800.

  • 35,000louis d’or, the cargo by Murray’s count and Dr Cameron’s; Murray says six casks
  • 800the loss at the landing in the Memorial; Murray puts the missing bag at 700 or 800
  • 12,981louis that Cluny’s statement of September 1749 lists as paid out, as the Memorial reproduces it
  • about 11,000the balance on the Memorial’s reading; a letter of 1751 reads the 12,981 as the sum still left

Of the 35,000, the Memorial accounts for about 11,000 in Murray’s custody: 800 stolen at the landing, 4,200 in back pay to units of the army, 3,000 lodged with an Edinburgh wine merchant, and 3,000 taken abroad with the Prince in September 1746. The other 24,000, it states, passed to Macpherson of Cluny. One account says Murray was arrested in June and held in the Tower of London. On 3 September he offered, from there, to travel under guard to the Highlands to recover £1,200, and an archivist’s catalogue entry asks whether this is the Loch Arkaig money; nothing there settles it. The Prince left Scotland that September, after a week or so sheltering with Cluny, who stayed on, hiding in the hills for about nine years, including a place on Ben Alder known as the Cage.

In September 1749 Cluny wrote out an itemised statement of the 12,981 louis he had paid out, and the Memorial reproduces it. One line, for taking in the money, included part of it that was, in his words, “lost and never recovered.” On the Memorial’s reading that leaves a balance of about 11,000. In Dr Cameron’s telling, Cluny said that month he would lodge 6,000 for the estate of Lochiel, Cameron’s elder brother, less 300 for his expenses, and keep the rest, saying the Prince had allowed most of it in writing. Cameron says he saw none but the 300. Cluny also wrote that people in the neighbouring districts had turned against him, because they believed he had kept the whole sum.

The accusations came three or four years after the landing. In January 1750 young Glengarry accused Dr Cameron of taking 6,000 louis. In March the Prince’s agent said the pair were “very flush of money.” Dr Cameron’s own Memorial says he took 300, for expenses. Other sources challenge both accusers: another correspondent accused the agent of losing 800 pounds at Newmarket, and in 1897 the writer Andrew Lang identified Glengarry as “Pickle”, a paid informer for the Crown. In May 1753 a correspondent named Edgar wrote that he wished with all his heart the government had got it in the beginning.

Dr Cameron was a physician and Lochiel’s younger brother. He returned to Scotland in 1753. Some accounts say he came to recover the gold; others tie the journey to the Elibank plot, a scheme to seize King George the Second, and Lang treats that as the context. He was arrested near Loch Katrine in March, and the tip-off is attributed to Glengarry. In June 1753 Cameron was hanged at Tyburn under the 1746 Act of Attainder, without a new trial. He is usually described as the last Jacobite executed for treason.

Cluny went to France in the mid 1750s to give an account. Lang says he carried “the slender remains” with him. Some later retellings say Charles accused him of embezzlement; Lang says no one but Glengarry and the agent questioned Cluny’s honesty. A different theory comes from an auction house’s press release for a silver cup. It says an Englishman, Charles Selby, from Northumberland, smuggled the gold to London through secret bankers, and that about 6,000 pounds reached the Prince. The release is a sales document, and its currency differs from the Memorial’s louis.

Two web pages say French gold coins were found buried in woods in the area in the 1850s, both citing Clan Cameron papers that could not be checked. In 2005, a museum was given a letter said to be an eyewitness account of the burial; a heritage website says it proved to be fake. That is one web page’s statement, and low in confidence. In 2020, people using metal detectors reported 215 musket balls, coins and gilt buttons from near Loch nan Uamh. One of them says the shot matches the calibre landed from the French ships. Published reports differ on what the coins were made of.

No modern search is reported to have recovered any of the gold. Whether the coins of the 1850s or those of 2020 had anything to do with it is not shown.

Six points are left open here rather than settled, because the sources read for this episode do not settle them. First, the documents themselves: Dr Cameron’s Memorial and Cluny’s statement were read in a transcript on a clan archive’s site, which is missing a page, and Murray’s memoir is the account of the man who held the money. Second, the dates of the landing and the sea fight, which the sources give differently; the video says the end of April (Murray’s “about a fortnight” after Culloden) where Dr Cameron’s Memorial says the beginning of May, and gives the sea fight as around the 2nd by one account and the 3rd by another. Third, the day of Dr Cameron’s hanging, which the sources do not agree on; the episode gives June 1753 only. Fourth, the total in the cargo, which sources give as 35,000, 36,000 or 40,000. Fifth, the 2020 find: no finds record, finders’ report or heritage-body entry was located, so the number and metal of the coins are not established. Sixth, the Selby theory, which rests on a seller’s press release.
