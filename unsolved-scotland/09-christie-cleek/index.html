The real record

What is actually documented, and what is not

In the summer of 1339 the Scots besieged Perth, a town held for Edward Balliol. The Guardian of Scotland led the besiegers, and the man holding Perth for Balliol surrendered the town. Walter Bower gives the date as 17 August. Andrew of Wyntoun and Bower both describe the district afterwards as ruined and emptied of people, and each reports many deaths from hunger. Wyntoun has deer coming close to the town, and Bower has beasts hunted around it; Bower adds that common people were found dead in pits.

About eighty years later, shortly after 1420, Wyntoun finished his history. His lines on the carl, an old word for a countryman, come in the passage on the siege and its aftermath, and run to some eight lines. They tell of a man who lived near there, as people told it, who killed children and women wherever he could catch them and ate the victims. His name was Christie Cleek, and he kept up that life while the country lay empty of people. The passage gives him no trade or wife, never mentions a capture, and places him only in the neighbourhood of the town. It cites its source in two words, “they said,” and names no one.

The next text found is Bower’s, begun in 1440. His sentence is short: near Perth a sturdy countryman named Christie Cleek lay in hiding with a woman, and they waited for women, children and young people and strangled them like wolves. In the text read for the episode, Bower gives no ending. The Pluscarden chronicle dates from 1461 and largely reworks Bower, so it may not be a third witness. Its entry reports a rumour that some people took up quarters in caves and ate children and women, names Cleek, with an old woman, as one example, and places this in the year after Perth was retaken, which would be around 1340. It ends the story, in translation: “in the end they died, being publicly handed over to justice and put to an ignominious death.” The word “they” is unclear: it may mean Cleek and the old woman, or the people in the caves in general. So Wyntoun gives a lone man, Bower adds a woman, and only the Pluscarden entry gives an end.

The earliest text found for the butcher, the Grampian cave and the hook is a tale credited to Alexander Leighton, in print by the 1850s at the latest. In the tale the man is Andrew Christie, a butcher originally from Perth, who lives with other destitute people in a cave near the Grampian Hills. A hook fixed on a pole gives him his name. An armed force is sent and several are caught, but “Christie and some others escaped, and were never captured,” which differs from Pluscarden. The tale counts the bones of no fewer than thirty travellers and claims the name has frightened children for five hundred years, without a source. Cleek is an old word for a hook; whether the name came first and the hook was fitted to it, the sources seen do not settle. Wyntoun and Bower both call him a countryman and Pluscarden names no trade, and a Grampian cave and a hook on a pole are missing from all three. What they share is a man, victims and a name; the butcher, the pole and the escape belong to the tale.

The historian Richard Oram calls Bower’s Cleek “probably mythical.” He also writes that tales of cannibalism in famines recur, with no trial papers behind them. The famine itself rests on Wyntoun and Bower: no independent evidence of one has been found, though the search was limited. What remains is a story set down long afterwards, by writers who describe a famine near Perth. What happened in those years, if anything, is unknown. Was there a man at all? The record does not say.
