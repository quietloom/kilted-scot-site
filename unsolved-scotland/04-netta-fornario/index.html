Episode Four · Iona · November 1929

Netta Fornario, Iona

The real record

What is actually documented, and what is not

In the late summer or autumn of 1929, Netta Fornario travelled to Iona with a large amount of luggage and rented a room. She was an occultist — a member of the Alpha et Omega, a secretive magical group descended from the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn — and had come, by later accounts, to make contact with what she called the island's fairy elementals. On or around 19 November she was found on the island's fairy hill, naked beneath a black cloak, a tarnished silver chain at her neck and a knife close to one hand.

The official certificate gives cause of death as exposure to the elements. It gives only a window for when she died: sometime between ten at night on the 17th and half past one in the afternoon on the 19th. Everything inside that interval remains unknown. No post-mortem or toxicology examination was carried out. Scotland had no coroner's inquest system; the death was referred to the Procurator Fiscal, and the available sources show no formal inquiry after that.

Most of the familiar story of her last day entered print in 1955, twenty-six years after her death, in Alasdair Alpin MacGregor's The Ghost Book, which drew on the memories of two women connected with Iona. It may preserve genuine events, but it is a late recollection, not a contemporary record. In that telling she became agitated on Sunday 17 November, said she was being attacked through telepathy or psychic means, and hurriedly packed to leave — but there was no Sunday ferry. She was reported missing on the 18th.

One contemporary report adds a last fact. The Oban Times said police removed a packet of letters from her possessions and sent them to the fiscal, describing them as “of a strange character.” No known source reveals what they said, what officials made of them, or where they are now.

What survives once the additions are removed is smaller: a woman disappeared after trying to leave Iona and was found exposed on a hill associated with fairy folklore. The recorded cause of death is exposure, and the weather on Iona in November makes that entirely credible. Hypothermia can produce confusion and, in some cases, paradoxical undressing, which could account for the condition in which she was found. It still leaves the preceding events obscure: her apparent panic, her walk into open country, and the absence of any medical examination that might have identified a contributing illness.

The theories offered since — a psychic attack, a medical crisis, fairy retribution, foul play — each fail in the same way: none can be tested against evidence that no longer exists. That missing evidence is what keeps the case alive. The legends grew around it. The record itself never grew at all.

The story says · the record shows

Each claim, and where it actually came from

As it circulatesVerdictIn the record
She died of heart failure. Wrong The certificate states exposure to the elements. It does not say heart failure, despite that claim appearing repeatedly online. Death certificate, as reported by the sources below
There was a coroner's inquest. Wrong Scotland had no coroner's inquest system. The death was referred to the Procurator Fiscal, and the available sources show no formal inquiry after that. The surviving record cannot tell us how much local work was done — only that no fuller public examination has been found. Episode research pack
Scratched feet, a rigid grip on the knife, terror fixed on her face. Later addition These details are absent from the earliest surviving accounts and appear later. The consistent, reasonably solid details are the cloak, the darkened chain and the knife within reach. Episode research pack
A cloaked man and blue lights were seen, by several witnesses. Unsupported The sighting traces to a single unattributed press line, not to corroborating witnesses. strangehistory.net, citing one unnamed newspaper report
A large cross was cut into the turf beside her. Doubtful The detail appears in occult coverage and later commentary; the death certificate says nothing about it, and at least one modern researcher doubts it was there. Episode research pack
Aleister Crowley did it. Wrong on the facts Crowley had no role in the Alpha et Omega and there is no documented connection to Fornario's death; his place in the story rests on a mix-up between famous occult names. No surviving source records a threat, struggle or injury pointing to murder. Episode research pack
This photograph shows Netta Fornario. Misidentified The photograph commonly labelled as Fornario shows Moina Mathers, another occultist linked to the same circle. No verified photograph of Fornario is known to exist, so the episode shows none. Episode research pack
Dion Fortune's account of a psychic attack is a neutral report. Mischaracterised It is Fortune's own account, in Psychic Self-Defence (1930), of a friend's death in occult terms. The figure it apparently implicates, Moina Mathers, seems to have died more than a year before Fornario, so the claim's chronology works against it. Dion Fortune, 1930; episode research pack

Some matters are left open here rather than settled, because the sources do not settle them: the exact date of the discovery (most accounts say the 19th, one source the 18th); which two local men found her, and on which day; and the order of her full name, which records give in different combinations of Marie, Norah, Emily and Editha. She was born in Cairo around 1897.

On the illustrations. Three shots in this episode are AI-generated — labelled on screen, for their full duration, as illustrations. They depict the later, unsupported additions to the story (a knife, a cloaked figure, a blue glow), never Fornario herself or any real person. Every other image is a real photograph, map or document, credited below.

  5. Jason Roberts, “The Immortal Hour of Netta Fornario”, Parts One to Three (2021) A three-part retelling used as a cross-check. Part Three draws on MacGregor's The Ghost Book and mentions the seized letters.
  6. Celtic Fire, “A terrible case of healing – the death of Netta Fornario”, View From The Big Hills (10 February 2013) The 1929 newspaper headlines, the letters passed to the Procurator Fiscal, and Ron Halliday's later reinvestigation.
  7. Mull Directory, “The Iona Enigma: Unraveling the Mysterious Death of Netta Fornario” (7 August 2025) Where she lodged, and the point on which accounts differ: a Mrs MacRae, or the Cameron family.
  8. Chris Merchant, “The mysterious death of Netta Fornario”, Coast that Shaped the World (Iona Abbey) An account from the island itself. It gives 17 November as the day she was found, one of the disagreements the episode leaves open.
  9. Find a Grave, memorial for Marie Emily “Netta” Fornario (1897–1929) The inscription on her grave at Reilig Odhrain: “M.E.F. 19th Nov 1929 Aged 33”.
  10. The National Archives, naturalisation record HO 144/1765/431695 (1922) Confirms her naturalisation in 1922 and the spelling of her names in the official record.

  12. Alasdair Alpin MacGregor, The Ghost Book (1955) The source of most of the familiar story of her last day, from the recollections of two women connected with Iona.
  13. Oban Times, 30 November 1929; Glasgow Herald and The Scotsman, 27 November 1929 The contemporary reports. The originals sit behind the British Newspaper Archive paywall, so they are cited through the secondary sources above and never reproduced.
