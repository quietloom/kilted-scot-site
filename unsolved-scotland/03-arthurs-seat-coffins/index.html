The Arthur's Seat Coffins

Researchers can tell you what they're made of and roughly when they were buried. Nobody has explained why — and the leading theory runs straight into evidence that doesn't fit.

The real record

What is actually documented, and what is not

In the summer of 1836, a group of boys hunting rabbits on the slopes of Arthur's Seat — the extinct volcano at the centre of Edinburgh — dug into the hillside and found a small opening in the rock, closed off by three flat pieces of slate stood on end. Behind the slate: seventeen miniature coffins, arranged in tiers, each carved from a single piece of Scots pine. Each coffin held a small wooden figure, a little over three inches tall, dressed in hand-stitched cotton clothes, with the stiff, upright posture and painted eyes of a toy soldier.

The boys' names were never recorded, and neither was how many of them there actually were. The first newspaper account didn't appear until three weeks later — The Scotsman, 16 July, placing the find on the north-eastern slope. The Caledonian Mercury put it on the opposite side of the hill that same summer. Two contemporary accounts disagreed with each other before the story even had time to settle.

Whatever the boys found, they didn't treat it carefully. The Scotsman's own account says several figures were damaged or lost outright — the boys used them as something to throw at each other. Of the seventeen coffins originally found, only eight would ever make it into a museum. The other nine simply vanished.

That detail comes from 1994, when researchers Samuel Menefee and Allen Simpson examined the surviving coffins closely and produced the clearest reconstruction yet. Their case: the figures weren't carved from scratch. They were most likely adapted from manufactured toy soldiers, made around 1790, decades before the burial, their arms removed to make them fit inside the coffins, then re-clothed and buried. Textile analysts Philip Sykas and Naomi Tarrant dated the thread used in the clothing to the 1830s, putting the actual burial within a few years of when the boys found it.

Nine years after the discovery, in 1845, the surviving coffins were sold at auction for four pounds, eight shillings. They eventually passed to a woman named Christina Couper, who donated eight of them to the National Museum of Scotland in 1901. They've been on display almost constantly since — among the most-visited objects the museum holds, sitting behind glass a short walk from the hill where they were found.

So what were they for? Witchcraft, a sailors' memorial, German mandrake talismans, a Burke and Hare commemoration — four separate theories, none of them fully fitting the evidence. The best-known modern theory has a hole nobody has closed: most of Burke and Hare's victims were women, but every surviving figure is dressed as a man. What's actually established is narrower than any single theory — someone with real skill converted old toys into burial objects in the early 1830s, and hid them with obvious intent. The how is reasonably well understood. The why has never been answered.

Each claim, and where it actually came from

As it circulatesVerdictIn the record
The coffins were carved from scratch for this burial. Probably wrong Menefee and Simpson (1994) argue they were adapted from a manufactured set of c.1790 toy soldiers, arms removed to fit. NMS's own wording is hedged — the figures “may have been” toy soldiers — not stated as settled fact. National Museums Scotland; Menefee & Simpson, 1994
They're the Arthur's Seat fairy coffins. Later invention No 1836 source mentions fairies at all. The framing is 20th-century folklore, now common in tourist material, not part of the contemporary record. Episode research pack
This is a Burke and Hare memorial. Overstated The best-known modern theory — but most of Burke and Hare's victims were women, and every surviving figure is dressed as a man. No 1836 source makes the connection at all; it's 1994 scholarship, not period belief. Book of the Old Edinburgh Club, Vol. 3, 1994
Nobody knows anything about who made them. Overstated the other way The manufacture window, the toy-soldier adaptation, and a shoemaker or leatherworker profile — recycled shoe-buckle latches, tool marks — are all reasonably well evidenced. What's genuinely unsolved is intent and identity, not method. National Museums Scotland; Menefee & Simpson, 1994
The witchcraft theory was a documented Scottish folk custom. Mischaracterised One newspaper's guess in 1836, not a documented practice — and objects made to curse someone are usually found damaged or destroyed as part of the ritual. These weren't. The Scotsman, 1836
Three 1836 newspapers each gave a different discovery location. Not quite Two contemporary accounts disagree — The Scotsman (north-eastern slope) and the Caledonian Mercury (south side). The commonly-cited third location, the foot of Salisbury Crags, comes from a later, undated Notes & Queries piece, not an 1836 source. Wikipedia; episode research pack

Two further disputes are left open here rather than settled, because the research did not settle them: exactly who bought the coffins at the 1845 auction (one source names Edinburgh publisher Thomas Clark, another calls the buyer unknown), and the exact number of Burke and Hare's victims (cited as both sixteen and seventeen across sources). Neither is asserted as fact in the episode.

On the illustrations. A small number of shots in this episode are AI-generated — captioned on-frame, for their full duration, as illustrations rather than photographs. They depict moments no photograph exists of: an anonymous maker's workshop, a research examination, an 1845 saleroom. No photographs of the 1836 discovery itself are known to exist.
