Episode Ten · Charlestown, Fife

Lime kilns by the Forth, on the Fife shore.

A harbour village on the Fife shore of the Forth, 17.4 miles by road from Edinburgh Waverley. Historic Environment Scotland describes its limekilns as the largest group in Scotland, and the records disagree about when they were built.

The real record

Beside Charlestown harbour stands a long bank of stone limekilns with arched mouths. Historic Environment Scotland (HES), in its scheduling record SM3734, describes them as the largest group of limekilns in Scotland. It records fourteen kilns, divided into an east and a west bank. All fourteen survive to their original height, though the two at the eastern end have collapsed internally and are unroofed, and the banks measure about 9 metres high and 110 metres long.

HES says the kilns operated until 1956. Fife Council's appraisal of the village says the Charlestown Lime Company operated them until they closed in 1956, after which all the buildings at the kilnhead were demolished and the equipment sold.

The information board at the kilns describes the process: limestone burned with coal, the quicklime shovelled by hand into rail waggons and taken to ships waiting in the harbour. A harbour board by the Inner Forth Landscape Initiative says the harbour “was once anything but quiet”.

The kilns and harbour are on the north, Fife, shore of the Forth. Our own route from Edinburgh Waverley to the harbour road came to 17.4 miles by road.

Three things the sources disagree on

01

When the kilns were built

The records give three different answers, so this page gives no single year. HES's scheduling record says the majority were built between 1761 and 1799. The former HES listing for the kilns says 1750–1760. The board on site says from the 1750s.

02

How much of the lime it supplied

HES says that at its peak Charlestown supplied 30% of the UK's building lime. The Scottish Lime Centre Trust says that at the height of its production in the early nineteenth century it supplied around one third of all the lime used in Scotland. The board on site says over a third of the country's lime.

Story vs recordThese are different shares of different totals (the UK's building lime, Scotland's lime) from different periods. We have found no primary source for the board's “over a third”.
03

When the village was founded

HES says the planned village was founded by Charles, 5th Earl of Elgin, with construction starting in 1756 at Double Row, with uniform workmen's cottages. HES's own scheduling record says the village was established around 1761, so HES gives 1756 in one record and about 1761 in another. Other sources differ on the start year too.

What you can see

Items marked “on our visit” are from our own filming on 4 and 5 October 2026, not from a source.

A

The kilns

On our visit we saw a long bank of stone limekilns with arched mouths beside the harbour. Several kiln mouths were fenced with iron gates, and safety barriers stood across two kiln fronts. We could not count the kilns on camera, so the figure of fourteen is HES's.

B

The harbour

On the morning of 4 October 2026 derelict boats lay in the harbour. Our owner saw a sign at the harbour entrance saying the harbour is no longer in use and warning against docking; we have no photograph of it and did not record its exact wording. HES records the harbour as silted and drying at low water, with the timber skeleton of a hexagonal harbour light, dated about 1900, at the end of the west pier. HES lists the harbour as Category B.

C

The village rows

HES lists the village rows (North, South, Double, Hall and Cross Row) as Category B. A typical row is a symmetrical run of six single-storey, three-bay cottages. HES calls them uniform; we do not say identical.

D

The Queen's Hall

Fife Council's appraisal says the carved window pediments and lintels of the Queen's Hall carry the name “The Queen's Hall” and the words “Erected in the Jubilee year of Queen Victoria”.

E

The Sutlery

HES records the Sutlery as built in 1770 as a Broomhall Estate granary and cart shed, and converted into the Sutlery store in 1804.

F

The Elgin Railway

HES says the Elgin Railway began in 1774 as a horse-worked wagonway. It says a surviving three-arch bridge dates from about 1795 and now carries a footpath, and that a scheduled tunnel runs north to south for 195 metres from the kiln-head to West Road, where there was formerly a bridge. We have not seen the tunnel and this is HES's record only; nothing we found says anyone may enter it.

G

The Lime Centre

The Scottish Lime Centre Trust says it was established in 1994 and moved into the Charlestown Workshops in 2002 after a three-year restoration.

H

Shipbreaking

Fife Council's appraisal says 500 ships were dismantled at Charlestown by a shipbreaking company that closed in 1962, including the scuppered German fleet raised from Scapa Flow. It adds that those were largely broken up at Rosyth, and only the remaining hulks were brought to Charlestown.

Attributed to one sourceThis rests on a single Council document and we have found no primary source behind it. The derelict boats in our footage are not claimed to be any of those ships.
