Episode Two · Dunbar, East Lothian

The boy who invented the propeller.

Sort of. A still-working harbour under a castle Parliament ordered pulled down, a quay full of creels, and a four-bladed propeller standing in for a local engineer the Admiralty turned away. Seven stops, all on foot.

The real record

Dunbar Harbour is a working port. Its commercial fleet lands mostly shellfish, and the harbour is looked after by the Dunbar Harbour Trust, a registered charity independent of the council. The boats tied up here are there to fish; that they also photograph well is a bonus.

It's really three harbours in one. The oldest, the Old or “Cromwell” Harbour, goes back to works that probably began some time after 1574. Victoria Harbour, now the main one, was begun in September 1842. Between them sits Broad Haven. Above all of it is what's left of Dunbar Castle, which the harbour-builders of the 1840s finished off by blasting an entrance through the rock it stands on.

Seven stops

01

The harbour itself

Three basins built centuries apart. The Old Harbour, on the east side, is the oldest; artificial works there most likely started some time after 1574. Victoria Harbour, on the west, was begun in September 1842, in the years when Dunbar still had a herring fleet to shelter. It's where the fishing boats tie up all year round now. The lifeboat station on the quay was built in 1901, but the town's lifeboat service goes back to 1808.

Story vs record“Cromwell Harbour” is the name everyone uses for the Old Harbour, and Cromwell didn't build it. The name comes from the story that his army used the harbour around the 1650 Battle of Dunbar, and from a £300 grant on record. The listed-building record says only that Cromwell “allegedly” gave it. The harbour's Wikipedia history goes further and calls the idea that his money started the harbour an “erroneous presumption”, because the works were there long before him. You'll still find it told the other way round, as if Cromwell built the harbour.

02

The castle above the quay

Dunbar Castle is closed to visitors, and it sits right behind almost every shot of the harbour. It was one of the strongest fortresses in Scotland. Black Agnes, Countess of Dunbar, held it against the Earl of Salisbury's siege in 1338. On 24 April 1567 the Earl of Bothwell brought Mary, Queen of Scots here. That December, Parliament ordered it slighted, pulled down so no one could hold it again. In the 1840s the new Victoria Harbour's entrance was blasted through the end of the castle rock. Part of the ruin fell into the sea in 1993, and it has been closed to the public ever since. Kittiwakes nest on the old stonework in the breeding season.

Story vs recordEveryone agrees Black Agnes held out. Nobody agrees for how long. Undiscovered Scotland says five months, the Harbour Trust says about six months, and Britain Express says six weeks. We've left the length out rather than pick one.

Photo: M J Richardson, CC BY-SA 2.0, geograph.org.uk via Wikimedia Commons

03

A bollard with a plan

A bollard on the marina quay has been painted as a Minion. That's the whole stop. It's in the video because it made us laugh, and we couldn't find out who painted it or when, so we won't pretend to know.

04

Robert Wilson's propeller

The big black four-bladed propeller by Victoria Harbour is a memorial, put up in 2003 for the 200th anniversary of Robert Wilson's birth. He was born in Dunbar on 10 September 1803. His father, a fisherman, drowned in 1810 during a lifeboat rescue, and Robert left school early for a joiner's apprenticeship. He grew up sculling in the harbour and built model boats driven by a rotating “scull” at the stern rather than paddle wheels. In 1827 a model reached the Earl of Lauderdale. In 1828 the Highland Society tested his design at Leith and gave him £10. A silver medal followed in 1832. When the idea went to the Admiralty, the Woolwich officers who looked at it sent back a short, unfavourable report, and that was that.

He did well anyway. From 1838 he managed James Nasmyth's Bridgewater Foundry near Manchester, and he gave Nasmyth's steam hammer its self-acting motion. He later became managing partner of the firm, by then renamed Nasmyth, Wilson & Co. In 1880 the War Department paid him £500 for his propeller improvements. He died at Matlock, Derbyshire, on 28 July 1882.

Story vs record“The boy who invented the propeller” is doing two jobs, and neither quite stands up. First, the boy: Wilson was sculling and tinkering as a child, but the model on record dates from 1827, when he was 23 or 24. Second, the propeller: the names usually credited are Francis Pettit Smith and John Ericsson, who both patented screw propellers in the mid-1830s, and the Admiralty went on to adopt Smith's design. Wilson's real claim is narrower, and still a good one: his working model was being shown and tested years before Smith's patent. He never got the recognition he felt he was owed, and he blamed Smith for it.

Photo: Jennifer Petrie, CC BY-SA 2.0, geograph.org.uk via Wikimedia Commons

05

The creel boats

A large part of Dunbar's fishing fleet is creel boats, and what comes ashore is mostly lobster, crab and prawns, with some whitefish. The stacks of creels on the quay aren't set dressing; they're between trips.

Worth knowingThe video's “around 33 boats” is the Harbour Trust's own count: 33 fishing boats, plus moorings for 45 leisure boats. It's the harbour's figure, and we couldn't find an independent count to check it against.

Photo: Jennifer Petrie, CC BY-SA 2.0, geograph.org.uk via Wikimedia Commons

06

Nauti

A seasonal seafood snack bar on the harbour, open in the summer months only. It's one of several small food stops the Harbour Trust lists around the quay. Its hours vary, so check before you go.

07

The wall of floats and boat boards

The last shot in the video is a long stone wall on the street by the harbour, hung with old buoys and floats, lifebelts and painted boat name boards. We couldn't find any documented history for it: not who started it, when, or which boats the boards came from. So this is a description, not a history. It's worth the detour, and worth reading slowly.
