Episode Fourteen · Newhaven Harbour, Edinburgh

The Fishwives' Harbour, inside Edinburgh.

A former fishing harbour on the Firth of Forth, now reached by tram. Historic Environment Scotland records a royal dockyard here from 1504. The lighthouse dates are another matter: the sources differ, and this page says where.

The real record

Newhaven is a district of Edinburgh, between Leith and Granton and about two miles north of the city centre. We always say “Newhaven, Edinburgh”: there is another Newhaven, in East Sussex, and the two are easy to confuse in a search.

Historic Environment Scotland's listing for the harbour (LB27956) says the village was made a Royal Naval Dockyard by James IV, and that the City of Edinburgh bought the village and harbour in 1510 to protect its port at Leith from competition. After the dockyard years it went back to being a fishing port. Oysters and herring were its trade, and the covered Fishmarket of 1896 followed.

Today the harbour is mostly used for leisure, with a little commercial fishing surviving. The Edinburgh tram line reached a terminus on Newhaven's eastern edge in June 2023.

Five things worth getting right

01

The two lighthouses

There are two on the breakwater, and they are easy to mix up. The domed stone building is the Old Harbour Light, listed as built about 1830, with a concrete dome in place of its lantern. The cast-iron octagonal tower is the New Lighthouse: Historic Environment Scotland gives 1869 for the tower, by James Dove & Company of Edinburgh, and 1878 for when it was first lit. The Chance Heritage Trust puts it at 50 feet.

Sources differThe Old Harbour Light's date is not settled: the listing says about 1830, and at least one other source gives a different year and designer. For the New Lighthouse, the sources do not agree on when it stopped serving as a navigation light, or on the year it was relit as a decorative one (2018 in one, 2019 in another). The relighting is described as decorative only. We give no single date for either, and nothing here should be read as one.
02

The breakwater

The listing records a breakwater of 1864, 540 feet long, enlarged in 1876, with a sea wall added in 1881. Other sources attribute it to David and Thomas Stevenson.

Story vs recordThe listing itself names a different Stevenson, who died in 1850, fourteen years before the work. That looks like an error in the record, and other sources point to David and Thomas, which is why we use their names. We have not seen the primary Northern Lighthouse Board papers, so the attribution rests on secondary sources.
03

The Fishmarket, and a museum that has closed

The covered Fishmarket on Pier Place dates from 1896 and is listed Category B (listed 28 November 1989). Historic Environment Scotland credits the idea to Henry Dempster, “the Ancient Mariner”, a native of the village: fish was to arrive daily by rail and be sold wholesale. The building was restored and converted in 1993, with a Heritage Museum added in 1994 by the listing's count.

Worth knowingThe Newhaven Heritage Museum is closed. Wikipedia, citing the Gazetteer for Scotland, records it closing in September 2006 with no plans to reopen, and we found no later source showing it open. We have not confirmed who occupies the building today, so check on the day before you plan around it.
04

The fishwives

Newhaven women sold fish door to door in Edinburgh from enormous wicker creels carried on their backs, in a tradition that ran from the 18th century well into the 20th. They were known for striped skirts and for the street cries “Caller Herrin'” and “Caller Ou”. David Octavius Hill and Robert Adamson photographed them in the 1840s, in some of the earliest social-documentary photography there is.

Hill and Adamson, “Newhaven Fishwives, Jeanie Wilson and Annie Linton”, calotype, c. 1845. Cleveland Museum of Art, 1987.18, CC0 (public domain dedication) via Wikimedia Commons. The names are the museum's title for the print.

Story vs recordThe end of the tradition has two dates. The Gazetteer for Scotland says the last fishwife retired in 1974; Threadinburgh says 1976. We say the mid-1970s and leave it there.
05

The wall cabinet at 5 Pier Place

Set into the wall of the tenement at No. 5 is a stone-cased cabinet carved with a sailing boat and inscribed “ERECTED 1775. RE-ERECTED 1900”. According to the local historian Peter Stubbs, who remembers it from the 1960s, it held a barometer, with a notice of high-tide times beside it. He also says the tenement was built around 1840 for the Society of Free Fishermen of Newhaven.

Single sourceThat description rests on Stubbs alone; Canmore returned an error when we tried, so we have no second source. We do not know where the 1775 cabinet stood before 1900 or why it was moved, so we do not say.
