Episode Eight · Pittenweem, Fife

A fishing harbour in the East Neuk, on the Fife coast.

A fishing village on the East Neuk coast of Fife, where Historic Environment Scotland says the name means “place of the cave”. This page sets out where the facts in the harbour videos come from, and where the sources disagree.

The real record

Historic Environment Scotland (HES) describes Pittenweem as a fishing village on the East Neuk coast of Fife, and says the name means “place of the cave”. It calls the village the East Neuk’s main fishing port in the 19th century.

HES lists Pittenweem Harbour as Category B, added to the list on 18 August 1972, and gives it a mediaeval origin. On the old east pier, HES says it was repaired and lengthened in 1656, much rebuilt in 1703 and repaired in 1724–26. HES says the harbour was enlarged and reconstructed in 1855, and that Sir John Anstruther greatly expanded it after 1770 for coal and salt distribution.

The Pittenweem Harbour Order Confirmation Act 1992 authorised an extension of the harbour breakwater ending 135 metres south-west of its junction with the existing breakwater, and describes the harbour as the recognised centre of the local fishing industry in the Pittenweem fishery district.

Trove’s harbour record places the Harbour Granary by the entrance to the west basin and records a fish market at the harbour, close to the Granary. HES lists the Harbour Granary as Category B, dates it to the late 18th or early 19th century, and describes it as three storeys, harled, with a forestair.

The Ordnance Survey 25-inch map layer for 1892–1905 marks a lighthouse with a flashing red light at the head of the east pier, and several beacons on the rocks south-west of the harbour, including one named Beacon Rock.

The church and the shore

HES lists Pittenweem Parish Church as Category A; its statement of special interest reads “For tower”. HES says the 1588 tower originally served as the tolbooth, and that its bell chamber and stone spire were added about 1630. It says the church may incorporate the “dortour” (dormitory) of the Priory.

On the shore, HES says 24 and 25 Mid Shore (Thistle House) was rebuilt in 1874, lists it as Category C, and describes an inset panel with a thistle and a date. HES says 7 Mid Shore (Larachmhor) was rebuilt in the late 19th century, its ground floor a public house modernised in a quasi-antique manner. The neighbouring 8–10 Mid Shore is late 19th century with an inset stone dated 1624.

HES dates the retaining wall to houses 1–19 West Shore mainly to the 18th century, describes it as an irregular tarred rubble sea wall with a slipway, and lists it as Category B.

On The Gyles, HES says 4–6 The Gyles is dated 1597 and was rebuilt in the late 17th century, and lists it as Category A. It describes 7 The Gyles as a 17th- and 18th-century sea captain’s house built on rock foundation to seaward. A Royal Commission (RCAHMS) caption on Trove says Gyles House and The Gyles were converted into modern housing in 1962 under the National Trust for Scotland’s Little Houses scheme.

Out to sea

The Northern Lighthouse Board says the Isle of May lies at the mouth of the Firth of Forth, five miles from the Fife mainland. It says the lighthouse was established in 1816 and credits Robert Stevenson as engineer.

The Neart na Gaoithe developers say the wind farm is 15.5 km off the Fife coast, that Scottish Ministers consented in 2018 to it being built with 54 instead of 75 turbines, that it first generated power into the national grid in 2024, and that ESB bought a 50% stake in November 2019. EDF reported on 25 February 2026 that it had reached commercial operation, and describes it as a 54-turbine project. The EDF post shows operation by that date, not an exact start date.

St Fillan’s Cave

HES describes St Fillan’s Cave as a natural underground formation divided into two compartments, with a well. HES says it was restored, and reconsecrated and dedicated to St Fillan, in 1935. HES calls the historical association with St Fillan unclear; see below.

The West Braes

The West Braes Project says the tidal pool is 19 m wide at the dam wall, is covered at high tide, and originally had a spring board, a chute and a pontoon. Mat Fascione’s Geograph caption dates the pool to about 1890. The Project says the pool’s original Victorian terraces were made accessible in the restoration, and lists repairs to the side access and retaining walls, a strengthened end dam wall, a new valve so the pool can be emptied for maintenance, repaired steps, and extra handrails right down to the beach.

The Office of the Scottish Charity Regulator (OSCR) records that the West Braes Project registered on 17 March 2017, as charity SC047249. As of 2 November 2026, the OSCR register says the Project was set up to maintain, run and make available to the public the crazy golf course and Pittenweem Tidal Pool at the West Braes. The Pittenweem community website says the crazy golf reopened in 2016, at the end of phase 1 of the project.

The Fife Art Trail credits the puffin, seal and whale panels of the tidal pool mural to the artist Paco Graff.

The Courier reported in January 2024 that the West Braes group had bought one of the 15 Wee Dug sculptures from the Scotties By The Sea trail. The Pittenweem Primary School and Nursery Parent Council says the name Bonnie Braes for the West Braes Wee Dug Scottie was chosen by the 3 and 4 year olds from the nursery.

The fishermen’s memorial

Crail Matters (21 October 2019) reports that the Pittenweem Fishermen’s Memorial Association was set up to commission, erect and maintain a tribute dedicated to the men and women who made their living from the sea, and also to those who lost their lives making their living from it. Crail Matters prints the address given at the unveiling of the memorial on 28 September 2019.

Things the sources disagree on

01

The east pier

HES says the old east pier was repaired and lengthened in 1656. An RCAHMS caption on Trove says the east pier was extended in 1641. The sources disagree, so this page gives no single extension date.

02

St Fillan

HES’s Bruce’s Wynd listing says the cave was “used as a chapel by St Fillan in the 8th century”. HES’s listing for the cave says “The historical association with St Fillan is unclear, and largely dates to 19th century accounts”. This page does not say Fillan used the cave, and does not say he did not.

03

Captain Cook and Charles II

An RCAHMS caption on Trove says Gyles House was the home of the Captain Cook who took Charles II to exile in France in 1651; the plaque on the house calls that story a 19th-century hoax. This page treats it as a story, not as fact.

04

The carved numbers

Trove’s harbour record, citing J Horsburgh and R McIntosh via Professor T C Smout (1996), says carved stone numbers on top of the east pier marked where and when fish would be sold and which boats were working.
