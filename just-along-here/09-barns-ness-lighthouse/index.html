Episode Seven · Barns Ness Lighthouse, East Lothian

A lighthouse that is no longer a light.

A tower credited to D A Stevenson, engineer, and no longer a working light. It stands on a protected coast, about 50 minutes by car and 32.6 miles from Edinburgh Waverley.

The real record

Wikipedia says Barns Ness Lighthouse was built between 1899 and 1901, and that construction took approximately two and a half years. The doorway lintel is dated 1901 (Wikipedia and the Chance Heritage Trust). A photograph of the doorway on Geograph (Richard West, CC BY-SA 2.0) shows a lintel inscribed “D A STEVENSON, ENGINEER” and dated 1901. Historic Environment Scotland's listing (LB1465), as reproduced by British Listed Buildings, credits “D A Stevenson, engineer, 1901”.

Wikipedia says D A Stevenson was a cousin of Robert Louis Stevenson. The lighthouse's former keepers' cottages stand beside it: Historic Environment Scotland lists a pair of single-storey cottages.

Our own route from Edinburgh Waverley to Barns Ness came to 32.6 miles and about 50 minutes by car (a Google Maps check by the owner gave 49 minutes and 32.9 miles). These are free-flow figures, so treat them as “about”.

What happened to it, and what is round it

01

No longer a working light

Wikipedia and the Chance Heritage Trust say the light was deactivated in October 2005 and that the lighthouse was put up for sale in 2006. The East Lothian Courier, in an undated article, gives 2007 for its being decommissioned, and describes it as owned by Lafarge and part of the company's Dunbar cement plant site.

Sources disagreeThe deactivation is October 2005 in Wikipedia and the Chance Heritage Trust and 2007 in the Courier. This page gives neither year as fixed, and makes no claim about who owns the lighthouse now.
02

A protected coast

The Barns Ness coast is a Site of Special Scientific Interest. The board on site says so, and so does Wikipedia's list of East Lothian SSSIs. A Scottish Geology Trust leaflet adds that hammering is not encouraged.

03

Fossils

The coast is rich in fossils. The board on site calls it “one of the best places in Scotland to find fossils”; the Scottish Geology Trust's page on Barns Ness is the second source. The Trust's leaflet says the limestone is full of fossils, including colonial corals, large solitary corals, brachiopod shells and crinoids.

Ages differThe Trust says the rocks are over 320 million years old, from the Carboniferous. The Scotsman says around 300 million years. We use the Trust's figure.
04

The walk

The Scottish Geology Trust says the short coastal walk from the White Sands car park to the lighthouse follows “arguably the best exposures of limestones in east central Scotland”. Walkhighlands and the Trust's leaflet both describe a Geology Trail of interpretation boards that ends at the lighthouse.

Attributed to one sourceThe “best exposures” line is the Trust's statement, and we have found no second source for it. It is about the walk, not the lighthouse.
05

Listed

Historic Environment Scotland lists the lighthouse together with its keepers' cottages and retaining walls as Category B (LB1465), added on 18 March 1986. The listing describes a tall, slightly tapering circular tower, rendered with long-and-short ashlar dressings, with a lantern of triangular lattice panes under a domed gallery top. The keepers' cottages are a pair of single-storey, flat-roofed cottages, each with a central door and two windows.

06

The tower and its light

Wikipedia and the Chance Heritage Trust both give the tower's height as 37 metres. The Chance Heritage Trust describes a white stone tower with a black roof. Both say the light was an isophase white every 4 seconds. The Chance Heritage Trust, which is an interested party because it is the heritage trust of the lens maker, says the lighthouse was fitted with a first order revolving optic supplied by Chance Brothers of Smethwick, and gives its manufacture date as 1900.

No range givenWikipedia's page gives the light's range two ways (10 and 8.7 nautical miles), so we give none.
07

Keepers to automation

Wikipedia says two keepers staffed the lighthouse until it was electrified in 1966. It stayed semi-automated, needing a single keeper, until 1986, when it was fully automated. The Chance Heritage Trust agrees on 1966 and 1986. The Chance Heritage Trust and Wikipedia both say it was machine-gunned in the Second World War and sustained no damage.

Not independentWikipedia's source for the wartime story is a Visit Scotland page, so the two accounts may share one origin.
08

The Flannan Isles link

The East Lothian Courier reported that Barns Ness features in the second season of the television series The Unexplained Files, in an episode about the disappearance of three lighthouse keepers from the Flannan Isles in December 1900. The Courier quotes the producer saying that both lighthouses were designed by David Stevenson; Wikipedia's page on David Alan Stevenson lists Flannan Isles (1899) and Barns Ness (1901) among his works.

09

Who built it

Historic Environment Scotland credits D A Stevenson, engineer, with the 1901 lighthouse. Wikipedia says it was built by the brothers David A. Stevenson and Charles Alexander Stevenson; the Chance Heritage Trust names “David Stevenson and Charles Stevenson”. The Courier says David Stevenson.

Wikipedia says David Alan Stevenson was born on 21 July 1854 and died on 11 April 1938, that he built 26 lighthouses in and around Scotland, and that his cousin was the author Robert Louis Stevenson. Each of those details rests on Wikipedia alone.

Sources disagreeThis page does not choose between a single designer and two brothers.
10

The rocks and the lime kiln

A Scottish Geology Trust leaflet from 2010 says the hollows on the shore, each about one metre across, are fossil tree-root sites in the limestone and “the most impressive geological feature of this trail”. It describes a thin coal seam in the small cliff above high tide, and a bed of solitary corals that was quarried as decorative marble for fireplaces over 100 years ago. It also says the lime kiln at the back of the beach was one of many where layers of coal and limestone were burnt to make quicklime for agriculture, a process it calls dangerous and labour-intensive that “proved its worth in increased crop yields”. The leaflet says Dunbar Cement Works, nearby, processes local limestone and mudstone.

Leaflet from 2010What the leaflet says about the kiln's condition, the cement works and the facilities was true in 2010 and we have not checked it since. Walkhighlands also says nine different limestones can be identified on the foreshore at Catcraig at low tide.
