Episode Six · Limekilns, Fife

A path of engraved stones that ends on a question.

A village on the Fife coast that was a medieval port for Dunfermline. It is about 35 minutes by car and 16.5 miles from Edinburgh Waverley, and the pier carries Beacon Way, a path of engraved stones whose last one asks what you will do here.

The real record

Limekilns is a village on the Fife coast, around 3 miles south of Dunfermline according to Wikipedia. The Historic Environment Scotland listing text for the pier (LB3729), as reproduced by British Listed Buildings, says Limekilns was the medieval port for Dunfermline. A local u3a history says it started as a safe landing place for pilgrims and supplies heading to the abbey and Royal Palace in Dunfermline.

Our own route from Edinburgh Waverley to Limekilns Pier came to 16.5 miles and about 35 minutes by car. That time is free-flow routing, not live traffic, so treat it as “about”.

Two things worth knowing

01

A right to hold a port, not a port

Around 1362, King David II granted Dunfermline Abbey the right to hold a port at Gellald (Limekilns) or at Wester Rosyth, with the consent of the owners. The Limekilns Heritage Trust gives the date as 1362, and a transcription of the charter on Electric Scotland gives the grant in those terms.

Story vs recordIt is easy to write this as “the Abbey was given a port”. The charter, as transcribed, grants a right to hold one, at one of two named places, with the owners' consent. The transcription's own header dates it 1363, so this page says “around 1362” and no more precise.
02

“Engraved stones”

Beacon Way is a path on the pier formed of engraved Caithness stones, carrying donors' names and inscriptions. The Heritage Trust's FAQ describes it as a proposed path 30 m by 2 m laid with engraved Caithness stone setts, so the size as built may differ. The Dunfermline Press quotes the Trust's chairman saying “we've got around 300 so far”; the article is dated “11th June” with no year, so that is not a current count.

Worth knowingWe say “engraved stones”, not “memorial stones”. The Trust does not use that word: donors pay to have a name or an inscription cut.

What we saw on our visit

These come from our own visit on 24 September 2026, not from a source. Where a text is quoted it is as we read it on the stone.

A

The closing stone

The last stone on the path reads “And now yours. What will you do here?”

B

The poem stone

An engraved slab in the grass carries this text: “Limekilns' rock-darkened harbour stands like a stanchion to the Inner Forth, mirroring its twin at Blackness across the estuary.”

Worth knowingWe could not find who wrote it or when, and the exact wording appears nowhere online that we could find. So it is quoted, not attributed, and the claim about a twin at Blackness is the stone's, not ours.
C

The RENOUF stone

Among the paving inscriptions is a stone with a couplet: “Limekilns Pier's fallen down! Fix it up with stone so strong — all day long!” We found no other record of it, so this is as we read it.

D

The beacon

A basket-topped beacon stands on the pier. A photograph catalogued on Geograph (6884792) calls it a beacon on Limekilns pier. Its caption says this beacon, a wooden post topped by an iron basket, is one of over 1000 such beacons placed around the UK to mark the centenary of the end of the First World War on 11 November 2018. That is the occasion the caption names, not a date for putting this one up. It has no plaque, and the Geograph record does not say where on the pier it stands, so we do not either.

More from the record

Each of these is attributed to the publisher that says it. Where only one publisher does, we say so.

03

The Ghauts

The Limekilns Heritage Trust home page says a ghaut is a cutting, and that The Ghauts is the name for the long cutting in a low ridge of rocks forming an entrance to the harbour. Wikipedia and a local u3a history also describe The Ghauts.

04

What the port carried

The Trust's history page infers that by the time of the 1362 grant the port “must already have been a going concern, with an established trade in wool, skin and hides”, and the Electric Scotland transcription of the charter also names wool, hides and skins. Wikipedia says ships traded with the ports of the Baltic Sea and France until the seventeenth century. At the end of the 17th century, the Trust's history page says, the harbour was “commodious” for shipping coal, and Roy's map of Scotland (1747-55) marks a pier in the middle of the bay.

Single sourceThe Roy map detail is on the Trust's history page only; we have not seen the map.
05

How it stopped being a port

The Historic Environment Scotland listing text, as reproduced by British Listed Buildings, says the harbour continued to be used as a commercial port until the mid 19th century, when it was superseded by the improved harbour at Charlestown. Wikipedia says that in the 1750s the lime industry moved about a mile west to Charlestown, and that the only tangible legacy of the industry in the village is its name.

06

The shelter wall

The Trust's history page, which reproduces a 2006 RCAHMS description, says the landward part of the pier is divided lengthways by a wall 109 ft long, built in 1870 to shelter craft moored on the east side.

07

A pier at risk

The Trust's home page says the pier is a category C listed building, listed on 19 December 1979, and that it is classified AT RISK on the Buildings at Risk register for Scotland, with its condition described as “very poor” and the risk category as “high”. We have not read the register itself. The Trust's undated FAQ says its immediate objective is to repair the collapsed end of the pier. That is the sourced background to the couplet on the RENOUF stone above, but neither statement tells you the pier's state today. The Trust's home page says local residents came together in 2015 to save the pier for the community.

08

Where the sources disagree

When the pier was built. The HES listing text, as reproduced by British Listed Buildings, says 18th century. The Trust's home page says the present pier was constructed in the 16th century, and its FAQ says the Halkett family built it in the 18th century for exporting coal. The Dunfermline Press calls it a “500-year-old pier”, and the u3a history ties the pier's present form to the 1931 Promenade.

How long it is. The Trust's home page says 250 metres. The Trust's history page, reproducing the RCAHMS description, gives its length as 230 ft on the west side but more on the east. A 2021 Fife Today article, which repeats the Trust's wording, says 250 yards.

Story vs recordThis page does not choose between these. Two of them are the same publisher disagreeing with itself.
