Episode Three · North Queensferry, Fife

The world's smallest lighthouse, give or take.

Right under the Forth Bridge, at the head of a Category A listed pier: a working light tower about five metres tall. How small it is, who built it and when it went dark all depend on which source you ask — so this page shows you the sources.

The real record

North Queensferry is the Fife end of the old Queen's Ferry crossing, and most of what's worth seeing here was built to serve that ferry: a long stone pier, a signal house, and a very small light tower at the pier-head. The ferry kept running until the Forth Road Bridge opened in 1964 and took its traffic.

The light tower is what people come for, and its record is untidy. Almost every visitor source — the museum's own listing, the Gazetteer for Scotland, local history sites — says it was built in 1817 by Robert Stevenson, the lighthouse engineer. The tower's own Historic Environment Scotland listing says something else: circa 1810–1812, designed by John Rennie, as part of the same ferry improvements that produced the pier and the signal house. One way to square it is that Stevenson's light was fitted to an existing Rennie structure in 1817 — but none of the sources we checked says that in so many words, so this page doesn't claim it. Both dates are in the table below, each with its source.

Five stops

01

The Fife Coastal Path

The fingerposts in the village say Fife Coastal Path, and this is where the path began: when it first opened, it ran from here at the Forth bridges round the coast towards the Tay. It was extended west to Kincardine in 2011; the official route now runs from Kincardine to Newburgh, about 117 miles, and it's one of Scotland's Great Trails. The westward extension is why Culross, Episode One, is on it too.

Story vs recordThe video calls North Queensferry the southern end of the path. It was, until the extension to Kincardine. Today the official route starts at Kincardine and passes through here, so the village is a stop along the way rather than the end.

Photo: Sandy Gemmill, CC BY-SA 2.0, geograph.org.uk via Wikimedia Commons

02

The Town Pier

John Rennie built the pier between 1810 and 1813, and Thomas Telford extended it between 1828 and 1834, so two of the era's big-name engineers worked on the same 165-metre pier. It's a Category A listed structure. For most of its working life this was the ferry's landing on the Fife side, and it's still where the Fife Pilgrim Way's North Queensferry stage starts (Stop 5).

Worth knowingNobody agrees on when the light went out. The Gazetteer for Scotland, queensferrypassage.co.uk and the Lighthouse Lowdown podcast all tie it to the end of the ferry in 1964. Cruise Forth and Welcome to Fife say that by its 2010 relighting it had been dark for 120 years, or around 100, which would put the end nearer the opening of the Forth Bridge in 1890. The sources don't settle it, so this page doesn't pick a year.

Photo: M J Richardson, CC BY-SA 2.0, geograph.org.uk via Wikimedia Commons

03

The Harbour Light Tower

A hexagonal sandstone tower about five metres high, topped by an octagonal glazed lantern and a copper dome with a cross-shaped vent pipe. The listing notes that the lantern and vent pipe are original. There are twenty-four steps inside up to the lamp. The light was an Argand oil lamp, and it was later converted to paraffin. Before the tower, the ferry light seems to have been shown from the Signal House next door: that building's own listing records that it was “also used for the purpose of a light-house”. The tower was restored and relit by the Princess Royal in June 2010. Per the operator's own page (undated), it's a tiny museum run by the North Queensferry Heritage Trust, free to enter and usually open daily, weather permitting. By arrangement with the Trust, you can light the lamp yourself and come away with a certificate as an honorary keeper.

Story vs recordThe video's title calls this the world's smallest lighthouse. The people who run it are more careful and call it the world's smallest working light tower, and the Lighthouse Lowdown podcast points out that the claim only holds under that exact wording. None of the sources we found cites an independent body that has actually ruled on it. The height is disputed as well. The listing, the Gazetteer and queensferrypassage.co.uk all give about 5 metres (around 16 feet), while Wikipedia gives 23 feet (7 metres). The figure on this page is 5 metres, the one the official listing gives.

Photo: M J Richardson, CC BY-SA 2.0, geograph.org.uk via Wikimedia Commons

04

Battery Road and the Briggers memorial

At Battery Road car park, at the Fife end of the rail bridge, stands one of a pair of seven-foot cast bronze memorials. The other is at the South Queensferry end. Alex Salmond, then First Minister, unveiled both in 2012. The memorial carries the names, ages and occupations of the 73 men and boys known to have died building the Forth Bridge, which opened in 1890. Local historians spent years tracing those names through the records. Around the base: “To the Briggers, past and present, who built, restored and continue to maintain this iconic structure.” The car park is also a stop on the Forth Bridges Trail, with benches facing the bridge.

Story vs recordThe official count at the time was lower: 57 deaths, the figure recorded when the bridge was finished. The 73 on the memorial came from local research more than a century later, which found deaths the original tally had missed.

Photo: Rosser1954, CC BY 4.0, Wikimedia Commons

05

Also, mile zero

A carved gateway board carries a Fife Pilgrim Way panel. The Way opened in 2019: 64 miles in seven sections, with two possible starts, Culross and North Queensferry, and a single finish at St Andrews. The North Queensferry section leaves from the Town Pier and runs 8.5 miles to Dunfermline, where the Culross section also ends. That puts Culross (Episode One), North Queensferry and Dunfermline, home of Pittencrieff Park (Episode Four), on the same route.
