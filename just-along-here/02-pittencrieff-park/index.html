Episode Four · Pittencrieff Park, Dunfermline

The park a billionaire gave away.

Half an hour from Edinburgh, across the Forth: the private glen Andrew Carnegie was barred from as a boy, bought in 1902 and handed to his home town. Seven real stops, all free to walk to.

The real record

For most of the 19th century, Pittencrieff Glen was private. It belonged to the Hunt family, Lairds of Pittencrieff, and it sat right against the Abbey and the ruined royal palace — ground the town had long thought of as its own. Carnegie's maternal grandfather, Thomas Morrison, and his uncles were among the local radicals who fought the Laird in the courts for access to it, and won.

The Laird's answer, as Carnegie tells it in his own autobiography, was to order that no Morrison be admitted to the Glen. Young Andrew, a Morrison on his mother's side, was shut out. Later accounts add that when the gates did open to the town one day a year, his family was still excluded. The boy looked in over the wall. The man, decades later and a year after selling his steel business to J. P. Morgan, bought the whole estate for £45,000 — the deal was confirmed to him by cable on Christmas Eve 1902 — and in 1903 handed it to Dunfermline along with a trust to run it. He called it the most soul-satisfying gift he ever made. The Carnegie Dunfermline Trust still owns the park; the local council has managed it day to day since 1976, and today that's Fife Council.

Story vs recordThe ban story is real, but almost all of its colour comes from one man: Carnegie himself, writing decades later in a memoir published after his death in 1919. The Trust's own page and later histories repeat it, and they agree on the core — a feud between the Laird and Carnegie's radical relatives, and a boy kept out. What nobody independent recorded at the time is exactly how the ban was worded or enforced. It's a family memory, honestly held, told by the family.

Seven stops

01

The Louise Carnegie Gates

The main entrance, at the corner of Bridge Street and Chalmers Street. Wrought-iron gates designed by Jamieson and Arnott, with the ironwork made by Thomas Hadden, and put up in the late 1920s. They're Category A listed, the top tier. The ironwork weaves together two sets of initials, AC and LC: Andrew's as well as Louise's.

Worth knowingThe “memorial” in the gates' official name is premature. They went up while Louise Carnegie was very much alive — she lived until 1946. And they aren't the gates the young Carnegie was kept out by: those belonged to the Laird, and these came a quarter of a century after the Laird had sold up.

Photo: Jim Barton, CC BY-SA 2.0, geograph.org.uk

02

The Andrew Carnegie statue

A bronze of Carnegie by the sculptor Richard Goulden, dated 1914, on a tall pedestal with a bronze plaque telling his life story. Goulden spent two years in Dunfermline as art adviser to the Carnegie Trust and also made the park's “Let Noble Ambition” fountain. The statue went up in Carnegie's own lifetime — he died five years later — and is Category B listed.

Photo: Jim Barton, CC BY-SA 2.0, geograph.org.uk

03

Pittencrieff House

The Lairds' own house, most of it built in the 17th century for Alexander Clerk of Pittencrieff. After the gift it became a museum, and Robert Lorimer remodelled the interior in the years before the First World War. It's Category A listed — and, per the Trust, closed to the public as of 2026. The museum shut after Dunfermline's Carnegie Library & Galleries expanded, and the Trust says it is working to get the house open again.

Worth knowingCarnegie wasn't the first Laird of Pittencrieff to make his name in Pittsburgh. General John Forbes, born in Dunfermline in 1707 and linked by both Carnegie and the Trust to this house, took Fort Duquesne from the French in 1758 and named the fort that replaced it after William Pitt. Carnegie was delighted by the coincidence: one Laird named Pittsburgh, and another made his fortune there.

Photo: Kim Traynor, CC BY-SA 3.0, Wikimedia Commons

04

Malcolm Canmore's Tower

A few courses of rubble on a rocky knoll above the Tower Burn: the core of the west and south walls of a rectangular tower with walls about ten feet thick. It's a scheduled monument. The tower is first mentioned around 1070, when Malcolm III married Margaret — the later chronicler Fordun describes the king's “fortified place” here.

Story vs recordThe video calls this older than the Abbey next door. The story is: HES dates Margaret's priory to 1070, the same year as the marriage. The stones are another matter. HES's own scheduling text says the masonry may be 12th–13th century; Canmore records a 1989 excavation that dated it later, probably 14th, which we have not checked against the record itself. On neither dating do the surviving stones clearly predate the Abbey church David I began in 1128. The royal site is older than the Abbey; the ruin you're looking at probably isn't.

Photo: Rosser1954, CC BY-SA 4.0, Wikimedia Commons

05

Tower Bridge

The bridge over the Tower Burn, at the foot of Tower Hill, once carried the main road into Dunfermline from the west. It's two bridges stacked on top of each other: a lower arch carrying a stone marked “A R” and 1611 — read by Canmore as Anna Regina — and an upper arch from the 1780s. Category B listed.

Story vs recordThe video's “one arch” bridge is a different, unconfirmed bridge. Tower Bridge itself has two arches, one above the other — about twenty feet apart, by Canmore's measurement. The two sources even disagree about the upper arch: the listing gives a panel reading 1780, Canmore reads it as 1788. Easy to walk past either way; it's the looking up that pays.

Photo: kim traynor, CC BY-SA 2.0, geograph.org.uk

06

Wallace's Well

The Scottish Cave and Mine Database describes a long disused well situated a little below the Tower Bridge at the foot of Tower Hill, and says it is said to have received its name from Sir William Wallace, who is alleged to have drunk from it. The database describes a 1.2 m square capped masonry enclosure with an iron grilled gate. Writing in 1844, the Dunfermline historian Peter Chalmers called it a famous well, much resorted to for its excellent water, and noted that Wallace, it is said, once drank a little of it out of the palm of his hand in the haste of a flight.

Story vs recordThe Wallace connection is disputed by the record itself. Ebenezer Henderson (1879) stated that the well was known as Spaw Well, or Well of Spaw, and suggested that this was easily corrupted into “Wallace Spa”; the Scottish Cave and Mine Database, the Northern Antiquarian and Atlas Obscura all report this. Atlas Obscura, a popular guide, says a nearby spring was called “Well O Spa” and that the name was more likely changed to trade on a local hero. The Northern Antiquarian reports a burgh council meeting in May 1773 complaining of a Mr Chalmers closing the waters, and says Patrick Geddes’s 1904 work gives before-and-after portraits showing how the well had been restored. Canmore classifies it as a 13th-century well and spa, and Fife Council’s heritage works list includes carefully excavating to identify the well.

Photo: Rosser1954, CC BY-SA 4.0, Wikimedia Commons

07

The Italian Garden

The video's closing shots are a bench and the Italian Garden. The Trust asked two big names, Patrick Geddes and Thomas Mawson, to plan the new park in 1903. Neither scheme was built as drawn, but both influenced the layout you walk through now.

Worth knowingHow big the gift is depends who's counting. Carnegie put the Glen at “between sixty and seventy acres”. Wikipedia and Parks & Gardens UK say 76. The Trust's own page says 80. Either way, it's big enough to lose an afternoon in.

Video 2: the quieter corners

A second video, “Pittencrieff Park, Fife | Big Country's First Gig”, was published on 19 October 2026. These are the real record behind it. Items marked “on our visit” are from our own filming on 11 and 16 September 2026, not from a source.

08

Vartan's Way

The tree-lined avenue that runs from the Louise Carnegie Gates up to the Andrew Carnegie statue. A plaque set into the ground beside the road reads “VARTAN'S WAY. IN RECOGNITION OF VARTAN GREGORIAN, 12TH PRESIDENT OF CARNEGIE CORPORATION OF NEW YORK, FOR HIS CONTRIBUTION TO INTERNATIONAL PHILANTHROPY, 2013”. A Brown University news article and a Carnegie Corporation article both report that in 2013 a path in the park was named Vartan's Way for Vartan Gregorian, then head of the Carnegie Corporation of New York.

On our visitOur owner photographed the plaque and filmed the avenue, and marked its position on a map: the road between the gates and the statue, with the plaque beside it. The articles name the path but we did not find them saying which one it is, so the location rests on our own photograph of the plaque.
09

The presidential bench

On our visit we read the inscription on a bench: “Unveiled by Matthew Barzun US Ambassador to the UK, on the occasion of the rededication of Pittencrieff Park, 1st September 2016”.

10

The telephone kiosk

A listed telephone kiosk dating from the 1920s. The British Listed Buildings entry, reproducing the Historic Environment Scotland (HES) listing, gives John Fraser, 1928, Category B.

Sources differThe park's own interpretive board, which we photographed on 16 September 2026, says 1929. We give both years.
11

The Japanese garden and its summerhouse

Pittencrieff Park has a Japanese garden. HES's garden inventory records one added to the Glen, and its listing for the rustic summerhouse (LB46940) says it is “located in what was designed as a Japanese garden”. HES lists the summerhouse as dating from 1906.

Worth knowing1906 is the summerhouse's date, not the garden's: we found no date for the garden, so this page gives none. The British Listed Buildings entry repeats the HES text, so it is not a second source for the 1906.
12

The Stuart Adamson bench and the Glen Pavilion

A memorial bench to Stuart Adamson overlooks the Glen Pavilion, where Big Country played their first live show on 4 February 1982. The Courier says the bench overlooks the venue; no source we found dates the bench itself.

Worth knowingWe say “first live show”, not “debut”, because “debut” can also mean the first record. The Pavilion now houses the Peacock Rooms cafe: on our visit on 11 September 2026 we saw the sign, and that is as of that date. Fife Council's Peacock Rooms page says that in good weather you can sit out on the terrace and enjoy the views across the Glen and beyond.
13

The peacock mural

On our visit we filmed a peacock mural on a wall in the park. A Dunfermline Press photo caption credits a mural at the park's peacock sanctuary to Donna Forrester, and the artist's own portfolio lists “Peacocks in Pittencrieff Park”. We compared the newspaper's photograph with our own still and it is the same wall.

Attributed to one sourceThe credit rests on the Dunfermline Press caption alone; the portfolio listing corroborates it but is not a second independent source.
14

The Sensory Garden

On our visit we filmed a planted corner of the park, which our owner labelled the Sensory Garden. The Friends of Pittencrieff Park confirm a Sensory Garden exists. We say nothing more about how quiet it is or where it sits.
