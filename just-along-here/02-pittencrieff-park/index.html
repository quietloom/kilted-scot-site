Episode Four · Pittencrieff Park, Dunfermline

The park a billionaire gave away.

Half an hour from Edinburgh, across the Forth: the private glen Andrew Carnegie was barred from as a boy, bought in 1902 and handed to his home town. Seven real stops, all free to walk to.

The real record

For most of the 19th century, Pittencrieff Glen was private. It belonged to the Hunt family, Lairds of Pittencrieff, and it sat right against the Abbey and the ruined royal palace — ground the town had long thought of as its own. Carnegie's maternal grandfather, Thomas Morrison, and his uncles were among the local radicals who fought the Laird in the courts for access to it, and won.

The Laird's answer, as Carnegie tells it in his own autobiography, was to order that no Morrison be admitted to the Glen. Young Andrew, a Morrison on his mother's side, was shut out. Later accounts add that when the gates did open to the town one day a year, his family was still excluded. The boy looked in over the wall. The man, decades later and a year after selling his steel business to J. P. Morgan, bought the whole estate for £45,000 — the deal was confirmed to him by cable on Christmas Eve 1902 — and in 1903 handed it to Dunfermline along with a trust to run it. He called it the most soul-satisfying gift he ever made. The Carnegie Dunfermline Trust still owns the park; the local council has managed it day to day since 1976, and today that's Fife Council.

Story vs recordThe ban story is real, but almost all of its colour comes from one man: Carnegie himself, writing decades later in a memoir published after his death in 1919. The Trust's own page and later histories repeat it, and they agree on the core — a feud between the Laird and Carnegie's radical relatives, and a boy kept out. What nobody independent recorded at the time is exactly how the ban was worded or enforced. It's a family memory, honestly held, told by the family.

Seven stops

01

The Louise Carnegie Gates

The main entrance, at the corner of Bridge Street and Chalmers Street. Wrought-iron gates designed by Jamieson and Arnott, with the ironwork made by Thomas Hadden, and put up in the late 1920s. They're Category A listed, the top tier. The ironwork weaves together two sets of initials, AC and LC: Andrew's as well as Louise's.

Worth knowingThe “memorial” in the gates' official name is premature. They went up while Louise Carnegie was very much alive — she lived until 1946. And they aren't the gates the young Carnegie was kept out by: those belonged to the Laird, and these came a quarter of a century after the Laird had sold up.

Photo: Jim Barton, CC BY-SA 2.0, geograph.org.uk

02

The Andrew Carnegie statue

A bronze of Carnegie by the sculptor Richard Goulden, dated 1914, on a tall pedestal with a bronze plaque telling his life story. Goulden spent two years in Dunfermline as art adviser to the Carnegie Trust and also made the park's “Let Noble Ambition” fountain. The statue went up in Carnegie's own lifetime — he died five years later — and is Category B listed.

Photo: Jim Barton, CC BY-SA 2.0, geograph.org.uk

03

Pittencrieff House

The Lairds' own house, most of it built in the 17th century for Alexander Clerk of Pittencrieff. After the gift it became a museum, and Robert Lorimer remodelled the interior in the years before the First World War. It's Category A listed — and closed. The museum shut after Dunfermline's Carnegie Library & Galleries expanded, and the Trust says it is working to get the house open again.

Worth knowingCarnegie wasn't the first Laird of Pittencrieff to make his name in Pittsburgh. General John Forbes, born in Dunfermline in 1707 and linked by both Carnegie and the Trust to this house, took Fort Duquesne from the French in 1758 and named the fort that replaced it after William Pitt. Carnegie was delighted by the coincidence: one Laird named Pittsburgh, and another made his fortune there.

Photo: Kim Traynor, CC BY-SA 3.0, Wikimedia Commons

04

Malcolm Canmore's Tower

A few courses of rubble on a rocky knoll above the Tower Burn: the core of the west and south walls of a rectangular tower with walls about ten feet thick. It's a scheduled monument. The tower is first mentioned around 1070, when Malcolm III married Margaret — the later chronicler Fordun describes the king's “fortified place” here.

Story vs recordThe video calls this older than the Abbey next door. The story is: HES dates Margaret's priory to 1070, the same year as the marriage. The stones are another matter. HES's own scheduling text says the masonry may be 12th–13th century; an excavation in 1989 went later, probably 14th. On neither dating do the surviving stones clearly predate the Abbey church David I began in 1128. The royal site is older than the Abbey; the ruin you're looking at probably isn't.

Photo: Rosser1954, CC BY-SA 4.0, Wikimedia Commons

05

Tower Bridge

The bridge over the Tower Burn, at the foot of Tower Hill, once carried the main road into Dunfermline from the west. It's two bridges stacked on top of each other: a lower arch carrying a stone marked “A R” and 1611 — read by Canmore as Anna Regina — and an upper arch from the 1780s. Category B listed.

Story vs recordThe video's caption says “one arch”. The record says two, one above the other — about twenty feet apart, by Canmore's measurement. The two sources even disagree about the upper arch: the listing gives a panel reading 1780, Canmore reads it as 1788. Easy to walk past either way; it's the looking up that pays.

Photo: kim traynor, CC BY-SA 2.0, geograph.org.uk

06

Wallace's Well

A long-disused, stone-lined well a little below Tower Bridge, at the foot of Tower Hill, with an iron grille over its mouth. Tradition, recorded by the Dunfermline historian Peter Chalmers in the mid-19th century, says William Wallace drank from it. Sheila Pitcairn's local history of Wallace in Dunfermline records that during work on Tower Hill in 1906 a cave was found, its entrance little more than a foot wide, with signs someone had once lived in it.

Story vs recordThe Wallace connection is disputed by the record itself. In 1879 Ebenezer Henderson noted the spring used to be called the Spaw Well, or Well of Spaw, and suggested “Wallace Spa” is just that name worn down in local speech. The 1906 cave is real, but only the Scottish cave register ties it to the well, and only as a belief. So: a genuine well, a genuine cave, and a hero's name that may be a mispronunciation.

Photo: Rosser1954, CC BY-SA 4.0, Wikimedia Commons

07

The Italian Garden

The video ends in the park's formal gardens, where it stops at a bench. The Trust asked two big names, Patrick Geddes and Thomas Mawson, to plan the new park in 1903. Neither scheme was built as drawn, but both influenced the layout you walk through now.

Worth knowingHow big the gift is depends who's counting. Carnegie put the Glen at “between sixty and seventy acres”. Wikipedia and Parks & Gardens UK say 76. The Trust's own page says 80. Either way, it's big enough to lose an afternoon in.
