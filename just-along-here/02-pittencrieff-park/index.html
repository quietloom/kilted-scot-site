Episode Four · Pittencrieff Park, Dunfermline

The park a billionaire gave away.

Half an hour from Edinburgh, across the Forth: the private glen Andrew Carnegie was barred from as a boy, bought in 1902 and handed to his home town. Seven real stops, all free to walk to.

The real record

Pittencrieff was a private estate that was open to the public one day a year. The Carnegie Corporation of New York, Historic Environment Scotland (HES) and the Carnegie Dunfermline Trust all say that Andrew Carnegie’s grandfather and uncles led a campaign for greater public access and that, as a result, Carnegie and his family were barred even on that open day. HES’s garden inventory names the Morrison family, Carnegie’s maternal relatives, as the family banned, and says they took the case to the House of Lords in 1862 and won. That year rests on HES alone, and the same text says James Hunt owned the estate “by 1867”.

Carnegie bought the house and estate in 1902 from Colonel James Maitland Hunt (Wikipedia and HES agree) and gave it to Dunfermline in 1903. HES says he gave it with an endowment of three-quarters of a million pounds to set up the Carnegie Dunfermline Trust, to bring “sweetness and light” to the people of Dunfermline. The Carnegie Corporation and the Trust both quote him calling it “the most soul-satisfying gift I ever made or ever can make”. The Trust owns the park and Fife Council maintains it. HES says that in 1976 the Carnegie Trust handed the park to Dunfermline District Council to run, the Trust remaining the formal owner.

Story vs recordThe ban story is told by the Carnegie Corporation, HES and the Carnegie Dunfermline Trust, and they agree on its core: a campaign for access led by Carnegie’s family, and a family barred from the grounds. These sources do not record how the ban was worded or enforced. It is a family story, told by the institutions that now run the park.

Seven stops

01

The Louise Carnegie Gates

The main entrance, at the corner of Bridge Street and Chalmers Street. The HES listing says the gates are by Jamieson and Arnott, 1927–29, with ironwork by Thomas Hadden. They’re Category A listed, the top tier. The ironwork weaves together two sets of initials, AC and LC: Andrew’s as well as Louise’s.

Sources differThe HES listing says the gates were erected while Mrs Carnegie was still alive, so the “memorial” in their name is premature; Wikipedia gives her dates as 1857–1946. On who made them and when, Wikipedia says the gates are by Robert Lorimer and were built in 1908, and the HES garden inventory says they were put up in 1928. This page follows the HES listing and records the disagreement.

Photo: Jim Barton, CC BY-SA 2.0, geograph.org.uk

02

The Andrew Carnegie statue

A bronze of Carnegie by the sculptor Richard Goulden, dated 1914, on a tall pedestal with a bronze plaque telling his life story. HES says the statue was paid for by public subscription and erected while Carnegie was still alive; he died in 1919. It is Category B listed. A photographer’s caption on Geograph, quoting the plinth plaque, says about 20,000 people attended the unveiling in 1914. HES’s garden inventory says the Ambition Fountain west of the house was sculpted by Goulden in 1908 and is listed B.

Photo: Jim Barton, CC BY-SA 2.0, geograph.org.uk

03

Pittencrieff House

The Lairds’ own house. HES’s garden inventory says it was built in 1610 and heightened in 1731; the listing says earlier-to-mid 17th century for Sir Alexander Clerk of Pittencrieff; Wikipedia says it was originally built in the 15th century by the Wemyss family. It is Category A listed, since 12 January 1971. The listing says that in 1904–05 the house was converted to a reading room and museum and that Robert Lorimer remodelled it a few years later; the dates given for his work are 1908–11 (listing), 1911 (HES inventory) and 1911–13 (Wikipedia). The Carnegie Dunfermline Trust’s Ambition page says the house has been closed to the public for several years; the page is undated.

Sources differThis page does not choose between the house’s build dates. HES’s garden inventory says Brigadier General John Forbes was born in the house in 1708, and that it was enlarged in 1740 with stones from the Dunfermline Palace ruins; Wikipedia gives Forbes’s birth as 1707. The Ambition page calls the house the former family home of General John Forbes.

Photo: Kim Traynor, CC BY-SA 3.0, Wikimedia Commons

04

Malcolm Canmore's Tower

HES’s scheduling text says the only surviving portion of the tower is L-shaped, measuring 13 m north–south by 4 m east–west, with foundations 2 to 4 m thick. The facing courses have gone, leaving the character of the building indeterminate. It is a scheduled monument (5 February 1992). HES says the site’s value lies in its probable association with the founding of the Royal Burgh of Dunfermline, it being thought to have been the residence of Malcolm III, and that the city’s coat of arms shows a round tower traditionally said to represent it. HES’s garden inventory adds that the tower is popularly regarded as Macbeth’s stronghold before Malcolm.

Story vs recordThe video calls this older than the Abbey next door. HES says Margaret founded a priory at Dunfermline in 1070 and that David I made it an abbey in 1128. The stones are another matter: HES says the masonry “may be of 12th to 13th century date”, while Canmore classifies the tower as “14th Century (Possible)”. This page records the two dates and does not choose; on neither does the surviving masonry clearly predate the abbey.

Photo: Rosser1954, CC BY-SA 4.0, Wikimedia Commons

05

Tower Bridge

HES lists Tower Bridge as a bridge of 1780 built above a 1611 structure, with parapets by James Shearer, 1915. It describes a “striking double bridge” that carried the Dunfermline to Stirling road across the Glen. HES says the original, lower bridge carried the main road west of Dunfermline across the Tower Burn, and that in the later 18th century the road was diverted away from the grounds of Pittencrieff House and the level of the bridge and approach road raised to give level access to the house. Canmore classifies it as a road bridge of three periods: 17th, 18th and 20th century.

Story vs recordThe video’s “one arch” bridge is a different, unconfirmed bridge. Tower Bridge itself is a double bridge, one structure above another. Easy to walk past either way; it’s the looking up that pays.

Photo: kim traynor, CC BY-SA 2.0, geograph.org.uk

06

Wallace's Well

The Scottish Cave and Mine Database describes a long disused well situated a little below the Tower Bridge at the foot of Tower Hill, and says it is said to have received its name from Sir William Wallace, who is alleged to have drunk from it. The database describes a 1.2 m square capped masonry enclosure with an iron grilled gate. Writing in 1844, the Dunfermline historian Peter Chalmers called it a famous well, much resorted to for its excellent water, and noted that Wallace, it is said, once drank a little of it out of the palm of his hand in the haste of a flight.

Story vs recordThe Wallace connection is disputed by the record itself. Ebenezer Henderson (1879) stated that the well was known as Spaw Well, or Well of Spaw, and suggested that this was easily corrupted into “Wallace Spa”; the Scottish Cave and Mine Database, the Northern Antiquarian and Atlas Obscura all report this. Atlas Obscura, a popular guide, says a nearby spring was called “Well O Spa” and that the name was more likely changed to trade on a local hero. The Northern Antiquarian reports a burgh council meeting in May 1773 complaining of a Mr Chalmers closing the waters, and says Patrick Geddes’s 1904 work gives before-and-after portraits showing how the well had been restored. Canmore classifies it as a 13th-century well and spa, and Fife Council’s heritage works list includes carefully excavating to identify the well.

Photo: Rosser1954, CC BY-SA 4.0, Wikimedia Commons

07

The Italian Garden

The video’s closing shots are a bench and the Italian Garden. HES says that in 1903 the Trust asked Thomas Mawson and Professor Patrick Geddes to prepare separate plans for the park, that both plans were acknowledged and shelved, and that the park was subsequently laid out by James Whitton, Superintendent of Glasgow Botanic Gardens.

Sources differHow big the gift is depends who’s counting. Fife Council, the Carnegie Corporation and HES give 76 acres; the Carnegie Dunfermline Trust’s Ambition page says 80. Either way, it’s big enough to lose an afternoon in.

Video 2: the quieter corners

A second video, “Pittencrieff Park, Fife | Big Country's First Gig”, was published on 19 October 2026. These are the real record behind it. Items marked “on our visit” are from our own filming on 11 and 16 September 2026, not from a source.

08

Vartan's Way

The tree-lined avenue that runs from the Louise Carnegie Gates up to the Andrew Carnegie statue. A plaque set into the ground beside the road reads “VARTAN'S WAY. IN RECOGNITION OF VARTAN GREGORIAN, 12TH PRESIDENT OF CARNEGIE CORPORATION OF NEW YORK, FOR HIS CONTRIBUTION TO INTERNATIONAL PHILANTHROPY, 2013”. A Brown University news article and a Carnegie Corporation article both report that a path in the park was named Vartan's Way for Vartan Gregorian, then head of the Carnegie Corporation of New York. Brown says Gregorian visited on Monday 14 October 2013 to plant a tree with a spade once used by Carnegie himself, and the Carnegie Corporation says the dedication was part of Andrew Carnegie International Legacy Week and the centennial of the Carnegie UK Trust. The Carnegie Corporation page carries a header date of 24 August 2015, but its text describes the 2013 events.

On our visitOur owner photographed the plaque and filmed the avenue, and marked its position on a map: the road between the gates and the statue, with the plaque beside it. The articles name the path but we did not find them saying which one it is, so the location rests on our own photograph of the plaque.
09

The presidential bench

On our visit we read the inscription on a bench: “Unveiled by Matthew Barzun US Ambassador to the UK, on the occasion of the rededication of Pittencrieff Park, 1st September 2016”.

10

The telephone kiosk

A listed telephone kiosk dating from the 1920s. The British Listed Buildings entry, reproducing the Historic Environment Scotland (HES) listing, gives John Fraser, 1928, Category B.

Sources differThe park's own interpretive board, which we photographed on 16 September 2026, says 1929. We give both years.
11

The Japanese garden and its summerhouse

Pittencrieff Park has a Japanese garden. HES's garden inventory records one added to the Glen, and its listing for the rustic summerhouse (LB46940) says it is “located in what was designed as a Japanese garden”. HES lists the summerhouse as dating from 1906.

Worth knowing1906 is the summerhouse's date, not the garden's: we found no date for the garden, so this page gives none. The British Listed Buildings entry repeats the HES text, so it is not a second source for the 1906.
12

The Stuart Adamson bench and the Glen Pavilion

A memorial bench to Stuart Adamson, who died in December 2001, overlooks the Glen Pavilion, where Big Country played their first live show on 4 February 1982. Wikipedia and the Courier say the bench was unveiled in September 2011, created and funded entirely by fans, with lyrics chosen in an online poll; the Courier names the Wigan fan Gwenda Matthews as its driving force and says The Skids played their second ever gig at the Pavilion.

Worth knowingWe say “first live show”, not “debut”, because “debut” can also mean the first record. The Pavilion now houses the Peacock Rooms cafe: on our visit on 11 September 2026 we saw the sign, and that is as of that date. Fife Council's Peacock Rooms page says that in good weather you can sit out on the terrace and enjoy the views across the Glen and beyond.
13

The peacock mural

On our visit we filmed a peacock mural on a wall in the park. A Dunfermline Press photo caption credits a mural at the park's peacock sanctuary to Donna Forrester, and the artist's own portfolio lists “Peacocks in Pittencrieff Park”. We compared the newspaper's photograph with our own still and it is the same wall.

Attributed to one sourceThe credit rests on the Dunfermline Press caption alone; the portfolio listing corroborates it but is not a second independent source.
14

The Sensory Garden

On our visit we filmed a planted corner of the park, which our owner labelled the Sensory Garden. The Friends of Pittencrieff Park confirm a Sensory Garden exists. We say nothing more about how quiet it is or where it sits.
