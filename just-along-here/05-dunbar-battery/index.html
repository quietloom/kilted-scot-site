Episode Five · Dunbar Battery, East Lothian

A gun battery with two birth dates.

A listed red-sandstone battery on Lamer Island, 30.5 miles and about 44 minutes' drive from Edinburgh. Historic Environment Scotland dates it to 1781; the board on the island gives the Napoleonic Wars. It is said never to have fired in anger.

The real record

Dunbar Battery is a listed gun battery on Lamer Island, at the mouth of Dunbar Harbour. Historic Environment Scotland (HES) lists it as “Lamer Island, The Battery” (LB24831, Category B, listed 5 February 1971), with the date of construction 1781 and Andrew Fraser credited, and says it was built in the later part of the American War of Independence. It describes a D-plan bastion, rounded on the north-west coastal side, of large squared sandstone, with sixteen pierced embrasure openings. A later two-storey house at the south corner is now a shell, with two embrasures altered for windows.

You get onto the island over a harbour bridge. The Dunbar History Society dates the harbour bridge to the early 1860s, probably 1861 (Wikipedia says 1860), and says the plan was drawn up by the brothers David and Thomas Stevenson, uncles of Robert Louis Stevenson. Today the battery is an open public space. The project was completed in 2017 according to four sources, though the Scottish Civic Trust says 2018, and it includes an amphitheatre whose steps are labelled with the names of Shipping Forecast sea areas.

Dunbar is on the same side of the Firth of Forth as Edinburgh, so the drive is along the East Lothian coast, not over the water. Our own route from Edinburgh Waverley to Lamer Island came to 30.5 miles and about 44 minutes by car.

Four things the sources disagree on, or don't say

01

When it was built

The HES listing gives the date of construction as 1781. The operator’s own access guide says “The Battery, completed in 1781”, and Fabulous North, Rankin Fraser, the University of Edinburgh book and Wikipedia (“from 1781”) agree; an HES blog says it was built in 1781 too. The operator’s on-site panel, “Battery Recharged!”, says “The Battery was built for the Napoleonic Wars and escaped attack”, with a timeline entry of 1803–1815.

Two statements from the operatorThe Dunbar Harbour Trust’s access guide and its on-site panel give different dates, and this page does not call either wrong. Canmore classifies the site as Battery (18th Century); we have not seen its detailed record.
02

John Paul Jones

An HES blog tells how in 1779 John Paul Jones led a small flotilla up Scotland’s east coast intending to take Leith and demand a ransom from its mayor and corporation, having learned that the Forth towns were vulnerable because their guns had been taken to London after Culloden. It tells the end of the raid as a “miracle”, a gale that came after a minister’s prayer at Kirkcaldy, which it presents as local tradition. The blog says Leith Fort was built in 1780 to a design by James Craig and that, in the following year, the Lamer Island battery was built to defend the entrance to the Firth.

Story vs recordThe blog tells the battery’s building as part of the response to Jones’s raid. The HES listing says only that it was built in the later part of the American War of Independence, and the Scottish Civic Trust and the operator’s access guide give privateers as the reason. The link to Jones is the blog’s reading, not the listing’s. We have also left out “father of the US Navy”, a title John Barry is given too.
03

“Never fired in anger”

The Scottish Civic Trust’s case study says the battery “never fired a gun in anger” and quotes the phrase without naming a source. The Dunbar History Society’s summary of a 2024 talk says a shot was never fired in anger from it. The operator’s on-site panel says the battery “escaped attack”, and also says Dunbar town was attacked twice during the American War of Independence, with a timeline entry of 1779 and 1781.

Worth knowingWe found no primary evidence either way. The sources say “in anger”, so we do too, and the claim stays attributed to them.
04

Who designed it

The HES listing credits “Andrew Fraser 1781”. British Listed Buildings reproduces the HES record, so this is one source, not two. We found nothing that says who he was, so this page says “listed as” and no more.

What you can see

A

The embrasures

HES counts sixteen pierced embrasure openings. Wikipedia describes a battery of 16 guns, which is not the same count. The wall faces the North Sea, and on our visit we saw that the stone is red sandstone.

B

The amphitheatre steps

The steps of the 2017 amphitheatre are labelled with sea areas from the BBC Shipping Forecast. On our own visit we read Forth, Tyne, Dogger, Humber, Thames and Dover among them. We have not counted the steps or confirmed that every one is a sea area, so we say “labelled with” and not “each named after”.

C

Sea Cubes

An artwork called Sea Cubes, by the artist Donald Urquhart, was commissioned as part of the battery improvements (Dunbar Art Trail, Scottish Civic Trust and Rankin Fraser all say so). The Dunbar Art Trail says the form of the cube echoes the embrasures in the battery walls and derives from the cubic form of a sea-salt crystal, alluding to East Lothian salt panning. Rankin Fraser describes stainless steel cubes engraved with foraminifera, micro-organisms found in the North Sea. These are two different descriptions, each from its own publisher.

The hospital years: sources differ

All of these sources say the site was a hospital. None of them agree on the dates, and this page does not choose.

HES listingA hospital in 1893 for infectious diseases, and a Casual Sick House and Military Hospital in 1906. Unoccupied by the mid 19th century (1853 OS map).
Operator’s access guideA two-storey hospital built within the walls around 1870; Dunbar Red Cross took it over in 1910; closed around 1920, and the building then gave emergency housing.
WikipediaAn infectious-diseases hospital established in 1874; closed in 1905/6; re-opened as a war hospital in 1914 and lasted to 1926/7, after which the structure was let as accommodation.
Dunbar History SocietyThe earliest mention of the hospital is in 1871.
Scottish Civic TrustAn infectious disease hospital, a convalescence hospital during the First World War, then social housing in the 1920s; empty for decades afterwards.
HES blogThe battery last saw use as an invalid hospital for soldiers during the First World War.

HES says a pier originally stood to the east and was washed away in a storm around 1914–18.

More from the record

05

Rebuilding it

The Scottish Civic Trust says the community consultation led to protecting the structure rather than fully restoring it, and that the main funder was Viridor Credits, which gave over £705K as one of three projects supported by its Legacy Scotland Fund in 2014. It says there was also funding from HES for repairs to the fabric and from East Lothian Council. The Trust says the battery was under a foot of soil and original flagstones turned up when the soil was stripped. Rankin Fraser, the lead consultant and contract administrator, says a fumigation oven and hospital foundations were found and kept in the design, and the University of Edinburgh book says the amphitheatre sits in the void formed by the walls of the former hospital.

06

The Creel Loaders

Nearby stands The Creel Loaders, a six-foot sandstone statue by Gardner Molloy of three figures: two fishermen loading a creel on a fishwife’s back, with two cats. Fabulous North and the Dunbar Art Trail say the cats commemorate the site once known as Cat’s Row, and that the statue came from the Dunbar Shore and Harbour Neighbourhood Group. Fabulous North says it was unveiled to the community in 2016. Both say fish lasses reputedly walked as far as Lauder to sell fish.

07

The harbours: sources differ

The Dunbar Harbour Trust describes the Old Harbour as 17th century; Wikipedia says artificial harbour works probably began after 1574, and calls the strong Cromwell association an “erroneous presumption”. Wikipedia says a port at Dunbar is first mentioned in a 1555 charter as Lamerhaven. Wikipedia says the Victoria Harbour was commenced in September 1842; Fabulous North says 1840.

08

Who looks after it

The operator’s access guide and the Scottish Civic Trust say the battery is owned by the Dunbar Harbour Trust and managed not-for-profit. The Dunbar History Society says the Trust was formed by Act of Parliament on 30 September 2004. Canmore lists the site as “Dunbar, Old Harbour; Cromwell’s Harbour; Broadhaven; Lamer Island Hospital”, classified as Battery (18th Century), Hospital (First World War) and Infectious Diseases Hospital (19th Century).
