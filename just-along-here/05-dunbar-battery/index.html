A gun battery with two birth dates.

A listed red-sandstone battery on Lamer Island, 30.5 miles and about 44 minutes' drive from Edinburgh. Historic Environment Scotland dates it to 1781; the board on the island gives the Napoleonic Wars. It is said never to have fired in anger.

The real record

Dunbar Battery is a listed gun battery on Lamer Island, at the mouth of Dunbar Harbour. Historic Environment Scotland's listing (LB24831) records it as built in 1781, in squared red sandstone, with sixteen gun embrasures originally. Two of the sixteen were later altered into windows, so a visitor counting open ones will find fewer.

You get onto the island over a lifting bridge from the harbour. That bridge is not the original fabric: the Dunbar history society dates the present drawbridge to about 1860–61, to a design by the Stevenson brothers. Today the battery is an open public space with an amphitheatre added in 2017, its steps labelled with the names of Shipping Forecast sea areas.

Dunbar is on the same side of the Firth of Forth as Edinburgh, so the drive is along the East Lothian coast, not over the water. Our own route from Edinburgh Waverley to Lamer Island came to 30.5 miles and about 44 minutes by car.

Four things the sources disagree on, or don't say

01

When it was built

Listed as 1781. That date comes from the Historic Environment Scotland record, and the British Listed Buildings entry, a Historic Environment Scotland blog post and a University of Edinburgh publication all repeat it. The board on the island itself, which the operator also publishes as a PDF, says something different.

Story vs recordThe site's own panel, headed “Battery Recharged!”, says the battery was built for the Napoleonic Wars, 1803–1815. Two tourism sites repeat the board. Our best guess is that the board is describing the later period when the battery was manned and conflating it with the build, but that is a guess. We have not seen the primary archaeological record (Canmore refused our request), so this page gives both dates and picks neither as certain.
02

John Paul Jones

In 1779 the Scottish-born naval captain John Paul Jones sailed into the Firth of Forth meaning to take Leith and demand a ransom. A gale drove his ships back out to sea before he could. Historic Environment Scotland's blog on Jones covers it.

Story vs recordIt is tempting to say the Dunbar battery was built to stop him. No source we found says that. The sources that explain why the battery exists point to privateers: the Scottish Civic Trust says it was a defence against sea-going privateers, and one local-history account (Moloney Archaeology) ties it to an attack in 1781 by a privateer under Captain George Fall. The Historic Environment Scotland post places Jones in 1779 and the Dunbar battery in the years after, which is a sequence, not a cause. We have also left out “father of the US Navy”, a title John Barry is given too.
03

“Never fired in anger”

Two secondary sources make this claim, the Scottish Civic Trust's case study and the Moloney Archaeology write-up, and neither cites anything for it. The operator's own panel says only that it “escaped attack”.

Worth knowingMoloney also describes the 1781 privateer attack as repulsed, and we could not establish whether the new battery took part. The sources say “in anger”, so we do too, and the claim stays attributed to them.
04

Who designed it

Historic Environment Scotland lists the architect or engineer as Andrew Fraser. That is a listing attribution from a single source. There was a Royal Engineer of a similar name who died in 1792, but nothing we found confirms they are the same man, so this page says “listed as” and no more.

What you can see

A

The embrasures

Sixteen gun openings were built into the wall, facing the North Sea. The stone is red sandstone, and you can see that for yourself.

B

The amphitheatre steps

The steps of the 2017 amphitheatre are labelled with sea areas from the BBC Shipping Forecast. On our own visit we read Forth, Tyne, Dogger, Humber, Thames and Dover among them. We have not counted the steps or confirmed that every one is a sea area, so we say “labelled with” and not “each named after”.

C

Sea Cubes

An artwork called Sea Cubes, by the artist Donald Urquhart, belongs to the site. Sources do not agree on how the cubes are decorated, so we describe nothing beyond the name and the artist.
