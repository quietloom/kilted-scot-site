Episode Fourteen · Kinghorn Harbour, Fife

A pier grant from 1698, on the Fife shore of the Forth.

A small harbour on the Fife shore of the Firth of Forth, 25.9 miles by road from Edinburgh Waverley. A pier grant is recorded there in 1698, the pier was in ruins by 1846, and the lifeboat station dates from 1965.

The real record

Graham’s Archaeological Notes (the Kinghorn entry) says no positive reference to artificial harbour works at Kinghorn has been found before 1609, when the harbours of Kinghorn and Pettycur were both in need of repair. The Historic Kinghorn report by the Royal Commission on the Ancient and Historical Monuments of Scotland (RCAHMS) says the same. Graham says a grant for a new pier was obtained in 1698, with work on it still unfinished in 1707; the Historic Kinghorn report gives the same.

Neither Roy’s map nor Ainslie’s (dated 1775 in the report) marks a pier at Kinghorn. Both sources say that may mean the 1698 pier had been ruined, or that the port had been superseded by Pettycur. Neither says which.

The Historic Kinghorn report says cartographic sources indicate a pier extending beyond the Kirk Craig in 1832, and that in that year it was ruinous and reputedly incapable of receiving or sheltering ships of any considerable burthen. Graham says the pier was in ruins in 1846 and the harbour “of little use” except for fishing boats, and that the remains of a dilapidated pier, very probably that of 1698, survive in the north corner of the bay below the town.

Canmore records Kinghorn’s “Kirk Harbour” as “probably 18th century”, consisting of “the rubble-quayed sides of a small bay” (J R Hume, 1976). This is the harbour we filmed. It is a different place from Pettycur, which has its own record.

Historic Environment Scotland (HES) lists the Harbour Road railway viaduct as Category C (LB46840, added 9 March 2000) and dates it to 1846. HES’s listing also says the Edinburgh & Northern Railway opened in 1847.

The Royal National Lifeboat Institution (RNLI) says Kinghorn’s inshore lifeboat station was established in June 1965 with a D class lifeboat, and that a new two-storey boathouse and launching ramp were built in 1995, with a new concrete slipway opposite. The RNLI says the Atlantic 85 class lifeboat B836 “Tommy Niven” went on service on 22 October 2009, donated by a bequest of Mr Thomas Niven. The station’s address is 30 St James Place, Kinghorn, KY3 9SU. Fife Council’s Common Good register (as at 31 March 2025) lists the Life Boat Station Building at that address as a Kinghorn Common Good asset.

Our own route from Edinburgh Waverley to Kinghorn harbour came to about 41.7 km (25.9 miles) by road, about 47 minutes with no traffic.

Things the sources disagree on

01

The Abden Shipyard’s dates

The Kinghorn Heritage Trail board says John Key bought the land from the Abden Estate in 1863, that the yard built 141 ships between 1863 and its “final closure in 1920”, and that the last ship launched there was the SS Kinghorn. The Kinghorn Historical Society’s web page says the yard was established in 1864 by John Key and “closed for the last time” in 1922, after 58 years. The two give different dates, so this page gives none.

02

Why there is no pier on the old maps

Graham and the Historic Kinghorn report both offer two possible reasons that Roy’s and Ainslie’s maps show no pier: the 1698 pier had been ruined, or the port had become subordinate to Pettycur. Neither source decides between them, and neither do we.

Not the same harbourPettycur is a separate harbour with its own record. Everything on this page about a pier, quay or bay is about the Kirk Harbour at Kinghorn.

What you can see

Items marked “on our visit” are from our own filming on 11 October 2026, not from a source.

A

The harbour and its board

On our visit we saw a hand-painted metal sign on a pebbledash wall reading “KINGHORN HARBOUR”, and stacked lobster creels against a rubble wall at the harbour. The Heritage Trail 1 board there is headed “The Old Town”, and its map has a “You are here” marker at the harbour end. Its credit reads “Interpretation board produced by The Royal Burgh of Kinghorn Historical Society”, with a 2019 copyright.

B

The railway viaduct

On our visit we saw a dark viaduct arch framing a harled house and the Firth. The Fife Council conservation area appraisal says views out to the Forth from the Trongate are framed by the viaduct arches.

C

The lifeboat station

On our visit we saw a wall sign reading “Lifeboats” with the RNLI flag, then “Kinghorn lifeboat station”, and an RNLI poster for the lifeboat B-836. A tractor was drawing an orange inflatable boat on a launch trailer across the sand. We name no owner for the slipway or the quay; the sources we read do not say.

D

The lifebuoy

On our visit we saw an orange lifebuoy housing reading “To save a life!” and “Installed in partnership with Coastal Communities Fund, Fife Coast & Countryside Trust, Fife Council”.

E

The old town streets

On our visit we saw a street sign on a white harled gable reading “East Gate”. The Fife Council appraisal says the conservation area is largely focused on the historic core “as defined by 18th century buildings”, including the High Street, the Nethergate leading to “the central harbour”, and Eastgate, Townhead and North Overgate. The Fife Council conservation area board on site says the town “traces its trading roots to the 6th century, much enhanced by Royal Burgh status prior to 1172”, and that its surviving historic centre dates from the 18th and 19th centuries, when linen and shipbuilding brought renewed prosperity.

F

The viewpoint railing

On our visit we read hand-painted white labels with arrows on the viewpoint railing: “Bass Rock”, “N/Berwick Law”, “Port Seton”, “Cockenzie”, “Ship Wreck”, “Inchkeith”, “Arthurs Seat” and “Leith”, with the arrows pointing outward over the water. We do not know who painted them, and we do not say which wreck the label means. The Fife Council appraisal says Inchkeith is visible over the rooftops of properties on Nethergate.

G

The foreshore

On our visit we saw foreshore sand and weed with the bay and houses on the far side of the beach, a black curved steel bench on a concrete landing below the cliff, and warm sandstone rocks with the sea beyond.
